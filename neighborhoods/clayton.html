Clayton Home Buying Questions, Answered With 2026 Sales

How much do houses cost in Clayton, Missouri?

In 28 single-family sales inside Clayton city limits that closed between January 14 and September 30, 2026, the median sale price was $1,240,000. The middle half sold between $960,000 and $1,816,250, and the full range ran from $530,000 to $2,595,000.

What does $1 million buy in Clayton?

8 of 28 sales (28.6%) closed at $1,000,000 or less. Of those 8, all 8 had three or more bedrooms, all 8 had two or more baths and 2 were ranch-style. Their median was 2,544 sq ft, built in 1947.

What does $1.25 million buy in Clayton?

15 of 28 sales (53.6%) closed at $1,250,000 or less. Of those 15, all 15 had three or more bedrooms, all 15 had two or more baths and 2 were ranch-style. Their median was 3,044.5 sq ft, built in 1935.

How fast do houses sell in Clayton?

The median sale spent 6 days on market (CDOM). A quarter of the 28 sales took 3 days or less; the slowest quarter took about 23 days or more. 5 sales took 30+ days, 1 took 60+ and the longest took 120.

See market time by price band →

Do Clayton homes sell over asking?

Measured against the original list price, 14 of 28 (50.0%) closed above it, 4 (14.3%) at it and 10 (35.7%) below it. The median sale was 100.1% of the original ask. From $1 million to $1,249,999, 5 of 6 sales closed above the original ask.

What kind of houses are common in Clayton?

Mostly houses from the 1920s through the 1940s. Of the 28 sales, 22 (78.6%) were built before 1960, 2 (7.1%) from 1980 to 1999 and 4 (14.3%) in 2000 or later. The median house was about 3,636 sq ft, built in 1936, and 2 of 28 (7.1%) listed a ranch style.

Does a 63105 or “Clayton” address mean the house is inside Clayton city limits?

Not on its own. All 28 single-family sales inside city limits were in 63105, but a ZIP code doesn’t confirm city limits. MLS listed “Clayton” as the city for 21 of them, “St Louis” for 6 and “University City” for 1. Check the St. Louis County GIS map for the exact address.

Methodology

How This Clayton Analysis Was Built

This analysis uses 28 closed-sale records from the STL Home Journey 2026 St. Louis County sales dataset (January 14–September 30, 2026). A sale counts here when its listing’s map coordinates fall inside the City of Clayton’s boundary, using the U.S. Census Bureau’s 2024 TIGER/Line place boundary. The same records feed the St. Louis County school district housing analysis.

  • Historical closed sales are not current inventory.
  • Days on market uses CDOM.
  • Sale ÷ original ask compares the close price with the original list price.
  • Ranch share counts any MLS style that includes “Ranch.”
  • Listing coordinates can be imprecise near the boundary. Verify jurisdiction and school assignment for the exact address.

The Honest Take

Small Geography, Large Regional Weight

Clayton is a compact city, but it does not behave like a small market. The city's own materials note a permanent population of 15,939 within about 2.5 square miles, while the daytime population swells above 46,000.

That tells you almost everything you need to know. Clayton is simultaneously a residential address and a regional employment and civic center. Buyers are paying for access, centrality, and a level of convenience that is hard to duplicate elsewhere in the county.

If a buyer wants the most space for the money, Clayton is usually not the answer. If the buyer wants one of the most central, efficient addresses in the metro, it very often is.

Location & Identity

Residential Prestige With a Working Core

The city describes Shaw Park as its oldest and largest park, at 47.47 acres, near the heart of Clayton's business district. That pairing of parkland and office core is a good shorthand for the city as a whole.

Clayton's appeal is not just residential prestige. It is the ability to live in a place that remains highly central to the region's daily professional and civic life.

Market Reality

What Buyers Actually Find Here

Clayton buyers are usually making a location-driven decision before they ever get to the house. They want the address, the proximity, and the efficiency of the city first.

That means the housing conversation can be unusual: condos, townhouses, and smaller-lot homes may make more sense here than they would in other suburbs, simply because the location value is carrying so much of the equation.

For the right buyer, that is exactly the point. Clayton is less about acreage and more about reducing friction.
