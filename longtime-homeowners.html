Longtime St. Louis Homeowner: Should You Stay or Should You Go?

This isn’t going to be an easy decision. Moving sucks. Repairs are expensive. And no one has a crystal ball.

The hardest part might be figuring out where to start.

Do you love your house? Are you married to the memories? Do you want your kids to inherit it? Are you staying because it still works for you—or because leaving feels impossible?

What would you actually miss if you moved—the house itself, or the life that happened there?

And if you knew your kids didn’t want the house, would that change your decision?

Those are real questions. And you can’t be afraid to answer them.

Once you do, you can start separating what you feel about the house from what the numbers, the house itself, and your options are telling you.

1 · Should I stay?

Should You Stay?

Let’s start with something that gets lost in almost every conversation about downsizing:

You don’t have to move.

If you like your house, can afford it, can maintain it, and it still works for the way you live, staying may be perfectly reasonable.

The question isn’t whether you’ve reached some age when you’re supposed to downsize.

The question is whether the house still works.

Think about how you actually live in it.

Are you using most of the house? Are the stairs becoming annoying—or genuinely difficult? Is the yard something you enjoy or something you dread? Are there rooms you haven’t really used in years?

Then think beyond the house.

Maybe your neighbors have become friends. Your doctor is nearby. You know the grocery store. Your church, family, restaurants and routines are all here.

Moving ten miles can change more of your life than the square footage suggests.

But staying isn’t automatically the conservative choice either.

Sometimes people remain in a house because moving feels overwhelming even though the house stopped making sense years ago.

That’s worth being honest about.

Ask yourself this: If you didn’t already own this house, would you choose it today?

That answer won’t make the decision for you.

But it tells you something.

2 · What staying costs

What Does Staying Actually Cost?

A paid-off house isn’t a free house.

You may not have a mortgage payment, but you still have property taxes, homeowners insurance, utilities, routine maintenance and eventually some expensive things that wear out.

  • Roof
  • HVAC
  • Driveway
  • Windows
  • Sewer lateral
  • Plumbing
  • Electrical

Maybe you’ve already replaced half of them. Maybe you’re looking around thinking the next ten years could get expensive.

That doesn’t mean you should sell.

It means you should compare the real cost of staying against the real cost of leaving.

Because the replacement isn’t free either.

A smaller house might cost more than you expect. A villa may have an HOA. A condo has fees. Renting eliminates some maintenance but introduces rent and future increases. Moving itself costs money.

And selling your current house has costs too.

Don’t compare your paid-off house to some imaginary cheap little place.

Compare it to the place you would realistically move into.

3 · Should I fix it?

Should You Fix the House?

DON’T REMODEL YET.

If you’ve lived in the same house for 20 or 30 years, there’s a good chance some of it looks dated.

That doesn’t necessarily mean there’s anything wrong with it.

An original kitchen can be dated and functional.

An older bathroom can be dated and functional.

Wood cabinets aren’t defective because white cabinets became popular.

There’s a major difference between dated and defective, and sellers can spend a lot of money confusing the two.

Before you start tearing things out, separate the house into three buckets:

  • Broken

    Something actually needs repair or replacement.

  • Buyer Problem

    Something could materially affect a buyer’s willingness or ability to purchase the house.

  • Dated

    Something works perfectly well but isn’t currently fashionable.

Sometimes spending money before selling makes sense. Sometimes a smaller repair produces a better result than a full renovation.

And sometimes the smartest thing you can do is leave it alone and price the house appropriately for its condition.

Before you spend $40,000 making the house look like someone else’s idea of updated, figure out whether you’re likely to get that money back.

4 · Walk away with

What Would You Actually Walk Away With?

Your house might be worth $400,000.

That doesn’t mean you have $400,000 to spend on the next chapter.

This is one of the most important numbers in the entire decision.

Start with a realistic estimate of what the house could sell for in its current condition.

Then work backward:

Expected sale price

−mortgage balance

−selling expenses

−repairs/preparation

−moving expenses

=approximate usable equity

Now compare that number with the realistic cost of your next housing option.

You might discover that selling unlocks enough equity to simplify your finances considerably.

Or you might discover that replacing the house you already own is considerably more expensive than you expected.

Either answer is useful.

You want to discover it before the For Sale sign goes in the yard.

5 · Where would I go?

Where Would You Go?

This question needs an answer before you get too excited about selling.

Not necessarily an address.

But at least an idea.

If you sold this house tomorrow, what would you actually want next?

  • A ranch?
  • A villa?
  • A condo?
  • A smaller single-family home?
  • An apartment?
  • Something close to your kids?

Maybe you want to stay close to the community you know. Maybe you want to move closer to your kids. Or maybe downsizing gives you the chance to consider parts of St. Louis you hadn’t considered before.

And don’t assume smaller automatically means cheaper.

The house you’ve owned for decades may have a very low mortgage—or no mortgage at all. Replacing it with a newer, smaller property can mean paying today’s price for the house plus HOA fees, higher insurance costs, different taxes or other expenses.

So don’t start with:How small should I go?

Start with:What do I want my life to look like after I move?

Then find the housing that fits it.

6 · How would I sell?

How Would You Sell It?

There isn’t one way to sell a longtime home.

And you don’t have to remodel the entire thing to qualify for a traditional sale.

  • Sell It in Its Current Condition

    Expose the house to the traditional market without major remodeling.

  • Make Selective Repairs

    Address specific problems that may materially affect the sale.

  • Renovate Then Sell

    Spend more upfront in an attempt to improve the eventual sale outcome.

  • Direct / Investor Sale

    Trade some market exposure and potentially price for convenience, speed or certainty.

Each solves a different problem.

The mistake is assuming “as-is” automatically means investor or “traditional sale” automatically means remodel first.

It doesn’t.

Your job is to understand what you’re giving up—and what you’re gaining—with each option.

7 · Helping my parents

I’m Helping My Parents

Sometimes you’re not the homeowner trying to answer these questions.

You’re the son or daughter.

And that changes the conversation.

Maybe Mom wants to stay and you’re worried about the house.

Maybe Dad is ready to move but has no idea what to do with 40 years of belongings.

Maybe assisted living suddenly became necessary and your family is trying to make several major decisions at once.

Or maybe you live three states away and somehow became the person responsible for figuring everything out.

Don’t turn it into one enormous problem called “sell Mom’s house.”

Break it into smaller decisions:

  1. Where is Mom or Dad going?
  2. What does the house actually need?
  3. What is it worth in its current condition?
  4. Would fixing anything materially change the outcome?
  5. What needs to happen with everything inside it?
  6. Who actually has authority to make decisions about the property?

And don’t start remodeling just because everyone agrees the kitchen is old.

Find out what the house is worth, what actually needs attention and what your parent’s priorities are first.

So, Should You Stay or Should You Go?

Maybe you stay.

Maybe you fix a few things and stay another ten years.

Maybe you realize the house has become more responsibility than home.

Maybe you sell it exactly the way it sits.

Maybe you finally buy that ranch you’ve talked about for years.

There isn’t one correct answer.

But there is a better way to make the decision.

  • Separate the memories from the condition of the house.
  • Separate the condition from the cosmetic stuff.
  • Figure out what staying actually costs.
  • Figure out what selling actually leaves you with.
  • And figure out where you’d actually go.

Then put those answers next to each other.

You don’t need a crystal ball. You need a clear picture of your options.
