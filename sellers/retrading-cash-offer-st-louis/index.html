What Is Retrading? When a Cash Buyer Lowers the Price After You Sign

Last updated October 13, 2026

George Kindler· Licensed Missouri Realtor· For Sellers

Retrading is the cash-offer problem many sellers have lived through without knowing the word for it. The buyer makes a strong offer, gets the house under contract, uses the inspection period to control the timeline, and then asks for a lower price before closing. The seller is left choosing between taking the cut or starting over after losing momentum.

Retrading, Answered

What is retrading in real estate?

Retrading is when a buyer goes under contract at one price and then asks for a lower price before closing, usually near the end of the inspection or due diligence period. It’s most common with cash investors and wholesalers.

Can a cash buyer lower the price after I sign?

They can ask whenever the contract gives them a way out. If it has an inspection or due diligence period, the buyer can usually cancel during it, and that right is their leverage to ask for a lower price. You don’t have to agree.

How do I prevent retrading?

What Retrading Looks Like in St. Louis

A buyer offers $145,000 cash and says the sale is as-is. The seller stops talking to other buyers. During a 14-day inspection window, the buyer brings in a contractor and shops the deal to other investors. On day 12, the buyer says the new number is $125,000.

A retrade is usually no surprise if the contract gave the buyer a long inspection period, tiny earnest money and broad cancellation rights. Those terms are what make it possible.

Doesn’t “as-is” mean they can’t ask for a lower price?

Not necessarily. “As-is” often means only that you won’t be asked to make repairs. If the contract still has an inspection contingency, the buyer can cancel and use that as leverage. Ask the buyer before you sign: “Does as-is mean no repairs and no price reductions after inspection, or only that you don’t expect me to make repairs?” Then make sure the contract says what they told you.

The Clauses to Negotiate Before You Sign

These decide how cheaply the buyer can walk away, and so how easily they can retrade:

  • Inspection period length. The longer the window, the longer the buyer controls your house while shopping the deal.
  • Earnest money. A buyer with little at risk loses little by renegotiating. On a cash deal, I recommend 10%, and more on an off-market offer. Why 10%.
  • Cancellation rights. Know exactly when and why the buyer can exit.
  • Assignment rights. A wholesaler who can’t find an end buyer at your price has every reason to retrade. How assignment contracts work.
  • Closing date. A firm date limits how long the deal can drift.

For the questions to ask and how to verify proof of funds, see how to vet a cash buyer.

What should I do if the buyer retrades?

Slow down and make the buyer show their work. Ask for the specific reason and the documentation behind the new number, such as the inspection report or contractor quote. Then compare it with reality:

  • Is the problem new, or was it visible when they made the offer?
  • Does the repair cost match what local contractors would charge? How to check a repair estimate.
  • What would the house net if you listed it instead? That’s your real alternative.

You have three choices: accept the new price, counter with your documentation, or decline and go back to market. Saying no is a legitimate answer. Before you decide, know whether you keep the earnest money if the buyer cancels.

Should I ever accept a lower price after inspection?

Sometimes. If the inspection found a real, material problem that any buyer would price in, a fair adjustment can still be your best path. That’s how I treat repair requests in a listed sale too: a real repair item is negotiated on its own cost. A cut that doesn’t match a documented problem is a retrade, not a repair.
