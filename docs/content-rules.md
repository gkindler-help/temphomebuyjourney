# STL Home Journey content rules

These are George Kindler's standing rules for every page on stlhomejourney.com. Apply them to new pages and to every page you rebuild.

## 1. Page structure: one topic, question subtopics, reader flow

Pages are built to be quoted by AI tools and still read well top to bottom.

- **One page, one topic.** The topic is the main thing a person is trying to figure out, such as "When should I lower my price?" or "Buying in Oakville."
- **Subtopics are the questions people actually ask.** Each one is a heading. Write it the way a person would type it.
- **Answer first.** Open with a sentence or two that can be quoted on its own. Then give the proof: a 2026 number, a table or a rule. Then George's take, in his voice.
- **Order the questions for the reader.** Follow the path a reader takes through the topic, for example the total, then each cost, then what you control. Don't use the order the questions were written in.
- **Articles follow the seller-page progression** (like "When to Lower the Price"): a boxed Quick Answers section at the top with about 5 headline questions and a one-line source note; then the article itself, with descriptive or story headings for the detail, tables, steps and George's examples; then a boxed "More Questions" section at the end, grouped under short labels, for the rest of the page's questions. Don't repeat a question from the top box in the end box or as a section heading.
- **Community and hub pages use the Oakville format:** one larger Quick Answers block near the top (about 8 to 13 questions), each with its own anchor id and a "See ... →" link to the detail section.
- **Every Quick Answers answer** opens with the direct answer and a specific number, source or rule. When another page owns the question, give the short answer and link to the owner.
- **Stories and data keep plain headings.** George's case studies, such as the Imperial listing, and data tables don't get forced into questions.
- **Data tables start collapsed (George, 2026-10-30: every table, not just big ones).** Every data table sits inside a closed "Show all N rows" toggle (`<details class="data-fold">`), so the page reads short on a phone. The rows stay in the HTML, so Google and AI crawlers still read and cite every number. Put the takeaway in a visible sentence above the table: the answer is never only inside the fold. After building or rebuilding a page, run `python3 scripts/fold-tables.py` (it skips tables a calculator writes and the sortable record on `/experience`).
- **Lessons From a Sale headings are questions (George, 2026-10-20).** Each section heading is a question that section answers, with the direct answer as its first paragraph. The end box keeps only questions that aren't already headings.
- **Each question lives on one page.** Its full answer and detail sit on the page that owns it. Another page's Quick Answers block can give a short answer with a "See ... →" link to the owner; anywhere else, give one line and a link. This prevents cannibalization.
- **Only add questions you can answer with evidence or George's own experience.** No padding. As a guide, focused articles carry about 12 to 18 questions, and community pages and hubs about 20 to 35.
- **No templated blocks across location pages (George, 2026-10-19).** Location + decision questions (MHDC, inspection, repairs, offer price, buyer representation) are written page by page. Each page gets only the two or three decisions where its own data stands out, asked from that angle, with links to different sections of the target guides. Don't give every page the same question set with the place name swapped. Before shipping a batch, check that the blocks don't share long repeated phrases.
- **FAQ schema matches what's visible.** FAQPage JSON-LD holds the questions in the top and end boxes (and any question headings), in reading order. No schema-only questions.

## 2. Data and accuracy

- Use only real data: 2026 closed sales, published sources or George's stated experience. Don't invent facts.
- No conflicting numbers anywhere on the site. When a figure appears on more than one page, it matches the page that owns it.
- Cite outside facts by name, for example "NAR's 2025 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers" or "Missouri DESE 2024–2025 APR."
- Label past sales as past sales, not current inventory.
- No "working to verify," "not confirmed" or "could not be verified" text on public pages.
- Fix typos and repeated sentences whenever you find them.

## 3. House style ("better safe than sorry")

- Don't make school-quality or reputation claims, and don't rank or editorialize about schools. Cited facts, such as DESE APR scores, are fine when stated plainly.
- Don't use "family/families" to describe neighborhoods.
- Don't steer buyers toward or away from areas, and don't disparage any area.
- Write "Mehlville R-IX" in full. Webster Groves is not South County.
- Don't cut content from pages that AI tools are already citing. Add to them instead.

## 4. George's positions (use his words)

- **Concessions:** never use one to generate a contract (never offer one on the listing). A buyer's own closing-cost request can be met when the price still delivers the seller's net, as at Autumn Ridge (George, 2026-10-27); that isn't using a concession to generate the contract. Concessions belong in repair negotiations after the inspection, where they typically run $2,000 to $8,000. Homes that sell over asking rarely give one unless there's a major repair.
- **Price reduction vs. concession or agent bonus:** lower the price. Buyers move first on price, then on inspections.
- **Property taxes:** listing taxes are the seller's most recent bill. Use them to compare areas, then confirm the exact address with the St. Louis County Collector of Revenue.
- **Tax figures come from confirmed taxes on the sales (George, 2026-10-22):** use the tax bills recorded on closed sales, the same bills closing prorations are figured on. Don't publish levy rates or third-party effective-rate estimates. A rate is that bill divided by the sale price.
- **Earnest money:** about 1% is typical for a retail sale with a financed buyer. On cash deals George has always recommended 10%, and sellers taking an off-market offer should ask for more.
- **Credit before closing:** George sends every buyer to a lender before they go under contract; the lender and George cover what not to touch on their credit. Hold big purchases such as cars and furniture until after closing.
- **Reserves:** keep six months of house payments after the down payment and closing costs, regardless of what the loan program requires.
- **Timing a purchase ("Is now a good time to buy?"):** no blanket answer; George is strategic by buyer. Stretching DTI in a competitive price range: wait for fall or look at listings that have sat. Strong borrower in a range with more homes: fall or spring. To compete less, narrow the must-haves. Selling first: list at the end of winter (February). Buying first: buy in spring, sell in summer.
- **Down payment assistance:** MHDC (First Place and Next Step) is the primary down payment program for St. Louis buyers; keep the focus there. Local City or County programs get a short summary, a "subject to change" note and a link to the program's own site.
- **Cash offers:** in George's experience, wholesale cash offers land around 50% of market value, rarely more than 60 to 65 cents on the dollar.

- **Area priority (George, 2026-10-15):** St. Louis County (South, West, Central) and Jefferson County first. North County and St. Charles County last, after issues are identified and resolved in the priority areas. In Jefferson County, fire and sewer districts drive many resale inspections; incorporated cities add their own occupancy rules, so every town page states incorporated/unincorporated and who inspects.
- **Repair cost ranges (George, 2026-10-17):** the ranges in `articles/stl-home-repair-cost-guide` and the home inspection tool are approved. Present them as **2026 expected costs**: the reader still needs contractor quotes, but a range is a great place to start a budget. Keep every page consistent with the guide. Don’t mention who reviewed them on the site.
- **Sewer lateral replacement (George, 2026-10-18):** never state it as a fixed range alone. Say it typically runs $4,000 to $12,000 and can exceed $15,000 in some cases. A sewer scope runs $150 to $300.
- **Sewer scopes (George, 2026-10-18):** recommend a sewer scope on every St. Louis home as part of due diligence, whatever its age or area. Don't limit the advice to older, pre-1980 or South City homes.
- **Seasonal timing (George, 2026-10-27):** say it plainly. The data supports buying in November, but the buyer may not find their house then, and outside circumstances such as a lease can prevent it. Demand and the median price peak in spring; if you don't like competition, spring isn't the time to buy. A house with an issue the seller can't control (slab, condo, main road, highway, mall) should be exposed when demand is highest. A limited budget in spring: know the market is in full force and search $25,000 to $50,000 below the maximum on list price instead of maxing out (Roy and Karen, Fenton San Luis: homes well below their $350,000 budget made them some of the strongest buyers in that pool). Owner page: articles/best-time-to-buy-sell-move-st-louis.
- **Offer price (George, 2026-10-19):** comps first, concessions second. "My position is always keep more cash in pocket, and stale listings are a great way to do it." Days on market sets leverage, not the opening number.
- **Escalation clauses (George, 2026-10-19):** use them with a hard cap in a true multiple-offer situation.
- **Appraisal gap guarantees (George, 2026-10-19):** it depends. In a real multiple-offer situation they reduce the seller's risk and can win the house, but don't increase the buyer's exposure when the competition may be inflated: in strong areas investors write non-retail offers, and listing agents can credibly say they have an offer in hand without calling it a junk offer. Only when the buyer's agent has asked the right questions, and only with cash to cover.
- **First offer (George, 2026-10-19):** it depends on the strategy. If listed at the top of the market and it hits the seller's goals, absolutely. Don't accept on the first evening; George calls and texts previous and upcoming showings and asks buyers' agents for 24 to 48 hours. "It's not about the first offer, it's about the motivated buyer." The first or second evening's buyer (listed Thursday) usually wins a multiple-offer situation.
- **Home-sale contingencies (George, 2026-10-19):** sellers who accept them are typically priced wrong; the buyer pays list or more to buy the risk. Listed = time-to-contract risk; under contract = risk doubles. Recommend waiting for other offers; they're great leverage. If one must be taken, ideally the buyer's home is under contract and past inspections; the listing agent runs a market analysis on it and talks directly with the buyer's listing agent.
- **FHA/VA buyers (George, 2026-10-19):** FHA and VA hold property to a higher (safety) standard than conventional, essentially the same guidelines, but the risk is always the individual buyer. VA buyers have much more room to get a loan commitment than FHA buyers.
- **Rent-backs (George, 2026-10-19):** "if this, then that." Ask if you need one, but risk cuts both ways and the seller may pay in price, concessions or rent. Everything in writing; stay true to the original contract's walk-through and damages; no second walk-through after the rental period; move-out falls under the rental period with seller responsibilities defined; once closed, it isn't renegotiated. Requiring one up front can stop offers; removing it has produced a contract within a weekend.
- **Earnest money when a buyer walks (George, 2026-10-19):** typically no, because most buyers release during their contingencies. When buyers fail to meet their contingencies or don't perform on the contract, George's sellers have kept it (tens of thousands of dollars). Never say "often yes." Release is by mutual agreement (the form is a mutual release); it's a contract and both sides can ask the other to perform. If the parties don't agree, RSMo 339.105.4 requires the broker to turn disputed funds over to the Missouri State Treasurer's Unclaimed Property Division within 365 days of the initial projected closing date (verified against the Missouri Real Estate Commission complaint notice). Owner page: `articles/lessons-from-a-sale-earnest-money-releases` ("Does the seller keep the earnest money if the buyer backs out?"). The offer page keeps a short answer that links there; the contingencies page owns the buyer's deadline question.
- **Earnest money release register, 2024–2025 (George, 2026-10-28):** seven signed seller awards, $5,725 total: 1209 Hamlet Ct $1,000 (Feb 2024, buyer couldn't close after the appraisal "didn't meet criteria"; not proof of a low value), 9738 Lorna Ln $500 (Mar 2024, first buyer; cause not documented), 171 Monteith Cir $1,000 (Aug 2024, investor buying 12 properties, extensions through Aug 9), 4126 Exeter Ave $1,500 (Aug 2024, first investor buyer on an estate sale; do not claim the novation questions caused the termination), 5635 Hodiamont Ave $500 (Dec 2024, original buyer never performed; replacement closed and funded Dec 19, 2024), 2832 Blackforest Dr $725 (May 2025), 1445 Fairmeadows Ln $500 (Nov 2025, buyer's lender not ready). The register covers 2024–2025 only; over 13 years George's sellers have kept tens of thousands of dollars. Counterexample: 6643 Chamberlain Ave (Mar 2024, listed around $165,000, March 4 contract, $1,500 earnest): title found a $40,000-plus mechanic's lien and an open loan; George asked the underwriter before the buyer paid for inspections; the Mar 14/15 mutual release returned all $1,500 to the buyer. Don't state the lien legally caused the release beyond the timing.
- **Bridge loans / HELOCs (George, 2026-10-19):** not very often; when used, work with an experienced lender and have the cash reserves to support two payments.
- **Buying with your own home-sale contingency (George, 2026-10-19):** "Just because I know what creates risk doesn't mean I wouldn't recommend it." It depends on the buyer's financial position and may be required. Know the risks: you'll pay more and have less leverage. First explore getting approved without it. "You can buy and sell quickly if your plan is defined and practical." No lender named; say "an experienced lender."
- **Repair vs. credit depends on the side (George, 2026-10-19):** never say "credits beat repairs" on buyer pages. Buyer side, matched to the item: (1) major items, anything affecting loan approval, occupancy or moving in without worry, get the repair completed before closing; a credit doesn't fix the issue and makes the buyer responsible for a repair that was on the seller. (2) Small items the buyer can live with temporarily: a credit or home warranty can make more sense than a handyman. (3) Serviceable items with no repair action, like a dated working furnace: no repair or credit is likely; ask for a home warranty. No prorated-credit ($300-$800 furnace) advice. Seller side: a credit usually beats a repair because George is reducing risk and making sure the deal closes.
- **Representation (George, 2026-10-19):** "I protect my client, while also being transparent and fair to the other party. But representation does not look like equal action or advice."
- **Buyer process (George, 2026-10-20):** before the lender, George has a phone call or in-person meeting (usually about an hour, because buyers have lots of questions): goals, what they want in a house, where they want to buy; he walks through the timeline and covers market health, showings, inspections, negotiating strategy and homes to target, then emails a summary with prep steps such as talking to a lender. "I'm on your timeline, I'm not rushing you." He hates hard sales tactics and sees himself as a counseling advocate; it's the buyer's purchase and the buyer is on the hook for the mortgage. "It's your job to fall in love with the house and it's my job to make sure you're not falling in love with a disaster," nor spending money when you shouldn't: he helps identify deal breakers before the inspection so the inspector looks at the systems, roof and under the basement floor, not the obvious. Before presenting an offer he calls the listing agent, makes them aware of conditions seen at the showing and asks whether the seller is open to repairs if the initial offer makes sense.
- **Buyer agreement and commission (George, 2026-10-20):** buyers sign a buyer agency agreement, "absolutely," but not before they're confident it's a good fit; George always offers to walk an open house together first; he isn't working as their agent there. It's an interview and educational, like his YouTube videos or this site, so no agency agreement is needed. The agreement is for "a rate that makes sense." Since 2013 his buyers have signed agency agreements with a stated amount. Before looking at a house he verifies with the listing agent whether and how much the seller offers in compensation. It's very rare for his buyers to pay the commission, not guaranteed; he focuses on the buyer's net number when presenting the offer, "not which column the debits or credits are on." Story: on a recent sale the seller wouldn't pay compensation even though the net made sense; the buyers paid it and they were all in at $323,000 with the commission, $27,000 under their $350,000 budget (921 San Luis Drive, Fenton; see `articles/lessons-from-a-sale-fenton-san-luis`). Don't say the settlement made buyers responsible for commission; say the agreement states the amount and the buyer owes whatever the seller doesn't cover.
- **Fixtures (George, 2026-10-20):** anything affixed to the house that isn't specifically excluded in the contract goes with the house (e.g. a Ring doorbell, spotlights). Sellers who want to keep one should exclude it in the contract or the agent remarks up front. Removing a fixture before closing is a material change in condition that can trigger the walk-through notice; the buyer can have the seller cure it before closing.
- **Client goals over districts (George, 2026-10-20):** much of the site is organized by school district, but George's focus is always the client's goals. Don't frame a buyer's choice as a district choice when the goal was location or the house.
- **Sunday listings and emotional roadblocks (George, 2026-10-20):** Sunday is traditionally a poor time to list because most buyers have already locked into an offer or set their showing schedule; the buyer who shows up that day is the motivated one. Sellers shouldn't introduce emotional roadblocks (countering the only buyer on commission, calling highest and best instead of negotiating with that buyer) any more than physical ones in the property.
- **First weekend (George, 2026-10-20):** "It's also my experience backed by market data: I get multiple offers the first weekend and rarely after that."
- **North County priority (George, 2026-10-20):** North County is now a priority. "SC is where I want to be, NC is where I should be able to get a leg up." The North County hub leads with George's experience there, a different approach from the South County hub. Lean on the career number (250+ transactions) and name the North County towns he has sold in; don't publish a North County sales count for now (George, 2026-10-20). Supersedes "North County last" in the area-priority rule.
- **Maryland Heights occupancy (George, 2026-10-20):** Maryland Heights requires its own occupancy inspection at every transfer of ownership; George had one on both 2026 sales there, and the city can be behind. Recommendation: when selling, schedule the occupancy inspection as soon as you plan to sell; it can take a few weeks in some cases.
- **Zestimate and Realist values (George, 2026-10-21):** misses run both ways (Zillow's median error is the middle of the misses, too high or too low, and Zillow doesn't publish the direction), but in George's experience Zillow and Realist values on St. Louis homes more often miss low. Present that as his observation, not Zillow data.
- **Turning off the Zestimate when selling (George, 2026-10-21):** yes, strategically. If the Zestimate is significantly below where the house is priced, George pulls it before listing; if it's higher, he leaves it up.
- **Jefferson County public records (George, 2026-10-21):** public information on Jefferson County homes is more limited. Many tax records reflect only the first floor, which understates square footage on two-story homes and skews price per square foot high. Check square footage against the listing or the house before relying on any online value or $/sq ft there. Zillow publishes Zestimate accuracy by metro only, not by county.
- **Waiving the financing contingency (George, 2026-10-21):** very rare. He has done it only for very strong borrowers who understand their earnest money is at risk and whose file an underwriter has already reviewed. Underwriting is two-fold, the buyer and then the property: an underwritten buyer doesn't guarantee the property will qualify, so the agent needs deep knowledge of conforming property requirements. When his clients have done it, it was usually on very clean properties.
- **Waiving the inspection (George, 2026-10-21):** George has had buyers waive inspections, and in this market he doesn't think one is a requirement. It takes an agent with deep knowledge of condition assessment; the risk is the unknown "beneath the hood" conditions such as the sewer lateral, radon and termites. He prefers a modified version: waive Option 3 and keep Option 2. The contract's inspection options are Option 1, approve and move forward (no action); Option 2, terminate without cause (the buyer provides the inspection reports if asked); Option 3, ask for repairs ("inspection resolution"). Other versions: a line-item option where the buyer inspects only specific items, which must be in serviceable condition; or a dollar threshold, with no request for any single item under $250. Supersedes "Should I waive the inspection to win? No." The sewer-scope rule still applies.
- **Multiple offers (George, 2026-10-21):** George rarely calls for highest and best. He prefers a hands-on approach: with the seller's consent, he takes each offer and pitches it to the next buyer. If the seller doesn't consent, he doesn't reveal a price. Wintergreen example: a second buyer asked whether the offer was over asking; the sellers declined to reveal price and terms but allowed "over asking." The second buyer never wrote. The contract was only $6,000 over asking ($406,000 on $399,900), and those buyers likely would have been in play had they known the amount.
- **Wintergreen was not an inspection waiver (George, 2026-10-21):** at 13218 Wintergreen Estates ($406,000 on $399,900), the buyers kept every contingency in the contract, including the inspection. Their agent verbally said they wouldn't ask for anything; George went on instinct about that agent and his knowledge that the house's condition wouldn't present issues, and weighed the offer as about equal to a $410,000 offer with a negotiation ahead. The buyers asked for no repairs. Never describe this sale as a waived inspection.

- **Lessons From a Sale answer pattern (George, 2026-10-28):** write every section for AI to cite. The heading is a general "how" question. The first paragraph answers it as a method anyone can follow (steps, rules, what to do), with no "we" story. Then a "How it went on this sale" block gives the specific facts as proof and links to the supporting page. Put one hard fact from the sale inside the method paragraph itself (AI cites the passage, not the link). Keep lesson headings specific to the situation so they don't compete with the guide page that owns the general question; the guide holds the general method and links to the lesson as proof. FAQ schema uses the method answer. Pilot: articles/lessons-from-a-sale-cedar-hill-creek-side (2026-10-28). Measure 3–4 weeks before rolling out to the other lessons. Lesson search titles (the <title>) lead with the searchable question and end with the place ("…? A Maplewood Sale"); the on-page H1 stays the story. Guide pages that AI already cites carry a one-sentence real-sale proof inside the cited answer, linked to the lesson (home inspection, cost to sell, buyer closing costs, repair costs; added 2026-10-28).
- **Lessons From a Sale headlines lead with the risk (George, 2026-10-30):** the H1 and the hub card title name the problem the reader hasn't thought to ask about (e.g. "A High VA Appraisal Can Still Miss What's Wrong With the House"); the deal's numbers go in the proof line under it (intro sub-line or lfs-h1-proof, and lfs-card-proof on the hub). The search <title> stays question-led. The hub introduces the series as the problems buyers and sellers don't see coming until it's too late.
- **What counts as a Lessons From a Sale page (George, 2026-10-29):** any page whose proof is George's own transactions, single sale or several. The series exists to quantify what the guides say with how it worked out in real life, so a page built on a set of George's deals (like the earnest money release register) goes in the series, with a lessons-from-a-sale-* URL and a hub card, not in the guides.
- **Names in Lessons From a Sale (George, 2026-10-28):** addresses are fine on transaction-archive stories, but never use personal names: no clients, buyers, sellers, agents, contractors, inspectors or title officers, and no team members by name ("my team"). Leave out private financial or personal circumstances (payoffs, divorces, family situations) unless George says otherwise. No free mentions of contractors or other vendors by company name (waterproofing, roofing, inspectors, title, lenders, brokerages) unless George says so (George, 2026-10-28).
