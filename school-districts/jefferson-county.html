Jefferson County School Districts: Can You Get the House Too?

By George Kindler · Published October 19, 2026

You found the school district you want.

Now comes the harder question:

Can you actually get the house you want there?

In 2026, a $300,000 budget reached 56.4% of Festus R-VI’s closed sales and 36.2% of Hillsboro R-III’s, two neighboring districts.

And sometimes spending more isn’t even the problem.

The house you want may simply not come up very often.

I analyzed 756 qualifying single-family resales across 12 Jefferson County school districts from January 1 through October 5, 2026 to answer a different kind of school-district question:

What did your budget actually buy—and what might you have to give up to stay in the district you want?

This Isn’t a School Ranking

I’m not going to tell you which school district to choose.

School programs, services, attendance boundaries and individual student needs deserve their own research.

This page answers the housing side of the decision:

What did homes cost? How much of the market was within a certain budget? How often did a particular kind of house sell? And what changed when buyers crossed a school-district line?

School assignments can vary by exact address. Always verify the assigned schools directly with the district before making a school-driven purchase decision.

Budget Example

Can I Buy a House in Fox C-6 for $250,000?

Yes—but the bigger question is how many chances you would have had.

From January 1 through October 5, 2026, 43 of 206 qualifying Fox C-6 single-family resales closed for $250,000 or less. That’s 20.9% of the market I analyzed.

So $250,000 did buy homes in Fox C-6, the district that covers Arnold and much of Imperial.

But it put a buyer into a narrow part of the district’s market.

At $300,000, the number increased to 87 sales, or 42.2%.

At $350,000, it increased to 117 sales, or 56.8%.

That’s the difference between asking “Can I buy there?” and asking “How much of the market can I actually shop?”

Budget Example

Is $300,000 Enough to Buy in Northwest R-I School District?

It reached half of the market.

In the study period, 76 of 152 qualifying Northwest R-I single-family resales closed for $300,000 or less. That’s 50.0%.

At $250,000, the share was 26.3%. At $350,000, it was 66.4%.

In this dataset, Northwest R-I took in most High Ridge, House Springs and Cedar Hill sales and part of Fenton’s, so a $300,000 buyer there was shopping the middle of the district’s closed-sale market, not the bottom of it.

Same Budget, Two Districts

Festus R-VI or Hillsboro R-III: Which Gave a $300,000 Buyer More Options?

In this dataset, $300,000 reached 56.4% of Festus R-VI’s qualifying sales and 36.2% of Hillsboro R-III’s.

That doesn’t make one district better.

It means the same budget occupied a different position in each housing market.

The pace was different too. Festus R-VI homes sold in a median of 28 days on market; Hillsboro R-III homes took 41.5.

Hillsboro R-III’s homes were also newer and larger on median: built 2001 and 1,851 square feet, against 1988 and 1,665 in Festus R-VI.

That’s the kind of difference a district median alone can hide.

Comparable Homes

Same House. Different District. Different Decision.

Let’s stop comparing a 1,000-square-foot ranch in one district with a 3,000-square-foot two-story in another.

For this comparison, I narrowed the data to one specific kind of house:

3 bedrooms · 2 bathrooms · ranch · 1,300–1,500 square feet

That’s a practical way to approximate a buyer asking:

“What would a roughly 1,400-square-foot ranch cost in different Jefferson County school districts?”

The answer isn’t just a price.

It’s also how often that house showed up.

Fox C-6 vs. Northwest R-I: What Did a Similar Ranch Cost?

In Fox C-6, 12 matching homes sold during the study period. The median sale price was $330,500.

In Northwest R-I, 6 matching homes sold. The median sale price was $289,250.

That’s a $41,250 difference in the median matching sale.

But price is only half the story.

The Fox C-6 data produced twice as many matching sales.

So a buyer choosing between these districts wasn’t only deciding whether Fox C-6 was worth paying more for.

They were also deciding how much a bigger pool of houses that fit the same basic description was worth to them.

Scarcity

Your Budget Might Not Be the Problem

Sometimes buyers keep raising their budget because they assume price is why they can’t find the right house.

But what if the house itself is scarce?

Jefferson County’s smaller districts make the point. Sunrise R-IX had 10 qualifying resales in more than nine months of 2026, and Grandview R-II had 9. You could have enough money to buy in either one and still wait a long time for the right house.

That is a different problem than being priced out.

A price problem can sometimes be solved by changing the budget.

A scarcity problem usually forces a different decision:

change the district, change the house, or accept that the search may take longer.

Competition

If the Right House Finally Appears, Will Everyone Else Want It Too?

Scarcity and competition are related, but they are not the same thing.

That’s why the tool also looks at days on market and sale price compared with original asking price.

Across whole districts, the pace varied widely:

  • Fox C-6: 9-day median CDOM, and 46.6% of sales closed above original list price.
  • Windsor C-1: 10.5-day median CDOM, 48.3% above original list.
  • Hillsboro R-III: 41.5-day median CDOM, 22.5% above original list.

For the roughly 1,400-square-foot 3-bed / 2-bath ranch, Fox C-6 had 12 matching sales with a 8-day median CDOM, and 58.3% sold above original list. Northwest R-I had 6, with a 3.5-day median CDOM and 50.0% above original list.

Those are small, specific cohorts, so don’t turn them into a prediction about the next house.

Use them as evidence that price, availability and competition are three separate parts of the buying decision.

Prices by Month

Did Jefferson County Prices Change Through 2026?

The median sale price moved between $274,900 and $325,000 from month to month. Price per square foot was steadier: it peaked at $179 in April and was $162 by September.

The busiest closing month was May, with 103 sales. The share of homes closing above original list price peaked in July at 50.5%.

Read the two charts together. The median sale price shows what a typical home closed for that month. Price per square foot strips out some of the size difference, because the mix of homes that close changes month to month.

With 57 to 103 sales a month, a few large or small homes can move a monthly median. Treat any single month as a rough reading, not a trend.

Budget Tradeoffs

What Happens If I Add Another $50,000 to My Budget?

Sometimes another $50,000 barely changes the search.

Sometimes it changes everything.

A few examples from the 2026 closed-sale data:

  • Hillsboro R-III: At $250,000, 9 of 80 sales (11.2%) were within budget. At $300,000, 29 sales (36.2%) were.
  • Dunklin R-V: At $250,000, 11 of 42 sales (26.2%) were within budget. At $300,000, 24 sales (57.1%) were.
  • Windsor C-1: At $250,000, 11 of 58 sales (19.0%) were within budget. At $300,000, 26 sales (44.8%) were.
  • Festus R-VI: At $250,000, 25 of 78 sales (32.1%) were within budget. At $300,000, 44 sales (56.4%) were.
  • Fox C-6: At $300,000, 87 of 206 sales (42.2%) were within budget. At $350,000, 117 sales (56.8%) were.
  • De Soto 73: At $150,000, 9 of 58 sales (15.5%) were within budget. At $200,000, 25 sales (43.1%) were.

The point isn’t that you should automatically spend another $50,000.

It’s to understand what the extra money actually buys you.

If another $50,000 barely changes your options, stretching may not solve the problem you think it will.

If it opens a much larger part of the market, at least you can see the tradeoff before deciding whether the higher payment is worth it.

Reading the Numbers

A Median Price Can Hide the Search You’re Actually Going to Have

Festus R-VI is a good example.

Its median sale price in this dataset was $292,500.

But 20.5% of its qualifying sales closed for $200,000 or less, while 15.4% closed above $400,000.

That doesn’t mean a $200,000 buyer had access to the same houses as a buyer at the median.

It means a single median number can hide a wide spread of condition, size, location and price.

That’s why this page shows both:

where the middle of the district landed and how much of the market actually fell below your budget.

The Decision

What Are You Willing to Change?

When the house you want doesn’t fit the district you want, there are only a few levers to pull.

You can spend more.

You can accept a different kind of house.

You can broaden the district.

Or you can wait.

The hard part is knowing which compromise actually changes your options.

The interactive should help answer that.

If adding $50,000 adds dozens of historical matches, that’s useful.

If it barely changes the search but broadening the square-footage range or crossing a district line changes it dramatically, that’s useful too.

The goal isn’t to push you toward the most expensive option.

It’s to show you what each decision buys you before you make it.

Boundaries

Before You Fall in Love With the House, Verify the Address

Jefferson County school-district lines do not follow mailing addresses.

  • Imperial: 57 sales in Fox C-6 and 39 in Windsor C-1.
  • Fenton (the Jefferson County side): 32 in Fox C-6 and 19 in Northwest R-I.
  • Festus: 75 in Festus R-VI, 19 in Jefferson R-VII, and 12 more across De Soto 73, Hillsboro R-III and Crystal City 47.

And MLS school fields can be wrong. During QA for this page, one Arnold sale listed with another district’s schools had to be corrected before the housing metrics were regenerated.

So use this page to compare housing markets.

When a specific school assignment matters to your purchase, verify the exact address directly with the school district before you write an offer.

Methodology

How I Built This

I started with a countywide MLS export of closed single-family resales in Jefferson County from January 1 through October 5, 2026.

The export covers vacant or owner-occupied homes. It excludes new construction and tenant-occupied properties.

Each sale’s school district comes from the high school listed in MLS. Fox C-6 includes both Fox and Seckman high school attendance areas.

Then I ran a separate QA pass. No public remarks showed a current tenant or lease, so no sales were removed for occupancy. One school-district misassignment was corrected after a geography review.

Two districts had a single sale each, Meramec Valley R-III and Kingston K-14. Those 2 sales are left out of the district comparisons because one sale can’t describe a market.

The final dataset contains 756 qualifying closed sales across 12 school districts.

For property taxes, I matched a second MLS export that includes each listing’s annual tax amount to this dataset by location, close date and price. 744 of the 756 sales matched with a tax amount. The tax is the seller’s most recent annual bill as listed in MLS, not a projection of the next owner’s bill.

How the numbers work

Median sold price is the middle Close Price in the district.

Budget share is the percentage of qualifying district sales that closed at or below the selected budget.

Comparable-house analysis uses the actual house characteristics in the dataset rather than estimating a price by multiplying a district-wide price per square foot.

The default “roughly 1,400-square-foot ranch” comparison means:

exactly 3 bedrooms · exactly 2 bathrooms · Ranch architectural style · 1,300–1,500 square feet

Days on market uses CDOM from the source data.

Sold above original asking compares Close Price with Original List Price.

Percentiles in the interactive (25th, 75th and the middle 50%) use linear interpolation between ranked sale prices, the same method as Excel’s PERCENTILE.INC.

What this data cannot tell you

This is historical closed-sale data.

It does not show today’s active inventory.

It does not guarantee that a similar house will become available.

It does not predict how long you personally will wait.

It does not determine whether a school district or individual school is right for you.

And it does not replace address-level school-boundary verification.

FAQ

Jefferson County School District Housing Questions Buyers Ask

What is the best school district in Jefferson County, Missouri?

There isn’t one school district that is “best” for every buyer, and this page does not rank districts by school quality.

If schools matter to your decision, research the individual schools, programs and current official performance information that matter to you.

What I can show you here is the housing tradeoff: what homes cost, how much of each district fell within your budget, what kind of house your money bought, and how often that house actually sold.

Which Jefferson County school districts had the lowest home prices in 2026?

By median closed single-family sale price in this dataset, the lowest-price districts were Sunrise R-IX, De Soto 73, Crystal City 47, Festus R-VI and Dunklin R-V.

That is a housing-price comparison, not a ranking of the schools or communities.

Can I buy a house in Fox C-6 for $250,000?

Yes, but it was a limited slice of the market in the period studied.

Of 206 qualifying Fox C-6 sales, 43 closed for $250,000 or less—20.9% of the district’s sales in the dataset.

At $300,000, the share increased to 42.2%. At $350,000, it reached 56.8%.

How much did a roughly 1,400-square-foot ranch cost in Fox C-6?

For the comparable-home cohort—3 bedrooms, 2 bathrooms, ranch style and 1,300–1,500 square feet—12 Fox C-6 homes matched.

Their median sale price was $330,500.

Use that price together with the count rather than treating it as a prediction of the next sale.

How did a similar ranch compare in Northwest R-I?

The same 3-bedroom, 2-bath, 1,300–1,500-square-foot ranch search produced 6 Northwest R-I sales with a $289,250 median.

Fox C-6 produced twice as many matching sales, at a higher median price.

Which school district is Imperial in?

Imperial is a mailing address, not a school district. In this dataset, 57 Imperial sales were in Fox C-6 and 39 were in Windsor C-1.

Verify the district for any specific Imperial address with the school district before you write an offer.

Does this page show homes for sale right now?

No.

This page analyzes closed sales from January 1 through October 5, 2026.

Current inventory changes every day.

The historical data answers a different question: whether your budget and house preferences were common or unusual in the market buyers actually purchased.

Are school-district boundaries the same as city or ZIP-code boundaries?

No.

In Jefferson County, mailing addresses such as Imperial, Festus, Fenton and Barnhart each cross more than one school district. Verify the assigned schools for any serious property directly with the school district before making a school-driven purchase decision.
