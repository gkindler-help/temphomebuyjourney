St. Louis Public Schools (SLPS)

Here's the first thing I explain to buyers looking in St. Louis City: St. Louis Public Schools and the school districts in St. Louis County are two completely different systems. The City of St. Louis is an independent city. It is not part of St. Louis County. If you buy a home inside the city limits, you're in the St. Louis Public Schools district, commonly called SLPS.

The second thing is just as important: knowing the district doesn't necessarily tell you where your child will actually attend school.

SLPS is much more dependent on magnet, charter and other school-choice options than many suburban districts. A home may have a neighborhood-assigned school, but that assigned school is often only the beginning of the school conversation. Families may consider magnet schools, charter schools, private schools or other available options instead.

That distinction matters when you're buying a house. I've worked in St. Louis real estate for 13 years and through more than 250 transactions, and I don't think buyers are helped by pretending the school question is simpler than it is. For some families, city schools are a genuine concern that changes where they're willing to buy. For others, understanding the available choices opens up neighborhoods they originally ruled out.

If you're thinking about buying a home in the St. Louis city school district, my goal here is to help you understand the system before you start judging houses solely by the school name attached to an online listing.

The real storyThe Real Story on St. Louis City Schools

If you search for the "best SLPS schools," you'll quickly find ratings, parent reviews, magnet-school discussions and strong opinions about St. Louis Public Schools. Some of that information is useful. Some of it lacks the context you need when making a home-buying decision.

There isn't one answer to whether SLPS will work for your family.

School quality and school fit can vary considerably from one option to another, and the choices available to a family can extend beyond the neighborhood school assigned to the house. That makes researching St. Louis City schools more involved than simply looking up a district and deciding whether you like it.

I also wouldn't ignore the concern. If having a specific traditional public-school path is one of your highest priorities, that needs to be part of your home search from the beginning. You may decide SLPS works for you after researching individual schools and options, or you may decide a suburban district is a better fit. Either answer is fine. The important part is making that decision based on the actual schools and enrollment paths available to your family rather than assuming every St. Louis address works like a suburban school district.

City vs. countySt. Louis City vs. County Schools: Where Buyers Get Confused

The word "St. Louis" causes a lot of confusion in real estate searches because St. Louis City and St. Louis County are separate jurisdictions.

St. Louis City has one city-wide public school district: St. Louis Public Schools. St. Louis County, on the other hand, contains roughly two dozen separate public school districts — around 23 traditional public districts, each with its own boundaries, administration and enrollment rules.

That means a listing with a "St. Louis" mailing address does not automatically tell you which school district serves the property. A home can have St. Louis in the postal address and still be located in St. Louis County rather than the independent City of St. Louis.

This becomes especially important for buyers comparing South City with South County. Crossing a municipal or county boundary can change the public-school district even when two houses are only a short drive apart.

It's one reason I recommend starting with location and school-boundary research before getting emotionally attached to a house. If you're still deciding between different parts of the metro, my where to live in St. Louis guide is a better starting point than trying to compare individual listings without understanding the geography.

How it worksHow SLPS Actually Works: Assigned Schools vs. Magnet and Choice Options

This is probably the most important section on this page.

When buyers ask me how SLPS enrollment works, they're often expecting the suburban model: buy this house, get this elementary school, then this middle school, then this high school. SLPS requires more homework than that.

An address can have an assigned neighborhood school, but that does not necessarily mean the child living there will ultimately attend that school. SLPS magnet schools are a major part of the system, and families may also investigate charter schools and other educational options available within the city.

Magnet schools are especially important to understand because admission and placement can involve an application process rather than simply living within a particular neighborhood boundary. Requirements, deadlines, available seats and placement procedures can change, so I would never recommend buying a house based on an assumption that your child is guaranteed admission to a particular magnet program.

That creates both an opportunity and a tradeoff. The opportunity is that you may have more educational choices than the neighborhood-assigned school shown next to a property online. The tradeoff is that school planning can require more research, applications and uncertainty than simply buying inside a highly sought-after suburban attendance boundary.

Charter schools add another layer. They are public schools but are not the same thing as an SLPS neighborhood assignment. Eligibility, admissions and enrollment procedures should be researched directly with the individual school and the appropriate official sources.

So when you see a school attached to a listing on a real estate website, treat it as a starting point, not a final answer. For a family with school-age children, I would research three things separately:

  • The neighborhood school currently assigned to the property.
  • The SLPS magnet programs that may fit the child and their current application requirements.
  • Charter, private or other school options the family would realistically consider.

That gives you a much more useful picture of what living at the address could actually look like.

AccreditationSLPS Accreditation: History and Current Status

SLPS has a notable accreditation history, and older articles about the district can create real confusion if you don't pay attention to when they were published.

The district lost full accreditation in 2007 over finances, student performance, attendance and dropout rates. It spent years under provisional accreditation, then regained full accreditation in January 2017 following improvements in academics, leadership stability and finances.

Here is the part buyers need to know today, because it is recent: on January 13, 2026, the Missouri State Board of Education voted 6-1 to downgrade St. Louis Public Schools from fully accredited back to provisionally accredited. So as of early 2026, SLPS is provisionally accredited.

What matters just as much as the status is the reason for it. Based on state board reporting, the downgrade was driven by governance and financial issues — frequent leadership changes, unresolved financial concerns, and repeated delays in completing a required annual audit — rather than by classroom academic scores. In fact, accreditation changes tied to academic performance under Missouri's MSIP 6 framework were not scheduled to take effect until 2027; this was an administrative classification change, not a test-score penalty. Provisional accreditation opens the door to closer state oversight.

Accreditation classifications can change, sometimes on short notice, so I always tell buyers to confirm the current status directly through the Missouri Department of Elementary and Secondary Education (Missouri DESE) rather than relying on an old news story, a social-media post, or a real estate listing. And I'd separate district accreditation from the question of whether a particular school is the right fit for your child — they're related, but they are not the same thing.

NeighborhoodsWhich South City Neighborhoods Does This Affect?

If you're buying inside St. Louis City, SLPS is the public school district regardless of which city neighborhood you choose. What can change by address is the neighborhood school assignment and the practical mix of school options your family considers.

That includes South City neighborhoods such as Bevo, Southampton, Dutchtown, Tower Grove East and Carondelet, along with the rest of the City of St. Louis.

Those neighborhoods can offer very different housing stock, prices, lot sizes, street layouts and access to different parts of the metro. That's why I wouldn't reduce a South City home search to a single school rating.

For example, a buyer comparing homes in Bevo and Southampton may care about the house itself, commute, price and neighborhood feel while planning to apply to a magnet program. Another buyer may put the neighborhood-assigned school near the top of the list. Those two families could look at the exact same properties and make completely different decisions.

If affordability is driving the search, use the home buying-power calculator before narrowing the map too far. Knowing your realistic payment and price range makes it much easier to compare South City homes with alternatives elsewhere in the metro.

ResearchHow to Research the Schools for a Specific St. Louis City Address

I recommend researching schools at the address level rather than assuming that every house in a neighborhood has the same situation forever.

Start with SLPS and confirm the current assigned school for the exact property address. Then look at Missouri DESE for official school and district information — DESE is where I would verify items such as accreditation and state-reported performance. GreatSchools can be useful as a secondary tool for organizing information and seeing individual school profiles, but I wouldn't treat a single GreatSchools rating as the entire answer; look at what goes into the rating and compare it with the official sources.

Then investigate magnet and charter options separately. If a particular school is important enough that it affects whether you would buy the house, contact the school or appropriate enrollment office directly and verify current requirements before making a decision. My basic process would be:

  • Confirm that the property is actually inside the City of St. Louis.
  • Verify the currently assigned neighborhood school for the exact address.
  • Review official Missouri DESE information for the district and individual schools.
  • Research SLPS magnet schools and current application procedures.
  • Research charter or private options if those are realistically part of your plan.
  • Confirm deadlines, eligibility and admission requirements directly with the appropriate school or enrollment office.
  • Do not rely solely on the school information automatically populated on a home listing.

Buyer tipsMy Buyer Tips for Purchasing in the St. Louis City School District

First, figure out how important the neighborhood-assigned public school is to you. There's a major difference between "we're open to magnet and charter schools" and "we want our kids to attend the public school assigned to our house." I need to know which buyer I'm helping, because those priorities produce very different home searches.

Second, research schools before you write an offer, not after. If your purchase depends on a particular educational option, verify it independently. I can help you understand the real estate and location, but I'm not going to tell you which school is "good" or make a school choice for your family.

Third, don't assume St. Louis County is automatically the answer just because the district structure feels easier to understand. Moving to the county can change the school options, but it can also change home prices, property characteristics, commute and the neighborhoods available within your budget. Compare the complete picture.

Fourth, don't automatically eliminate South City because you saw one school rating attached to a listing. Research the actual address and the choices available. You may still decide against it, but you'll be making that decision with better information.

After 13 years in real estate and more than 250 transactions, that's generally how I approach these decisions: understand what matters enough to change the purchase, verify the information that can change, and then compare the tradeoffs. Buying a home in St. Louis City with kids can require more school research than buying in some suburban districts. That doesn't make the decision impossible — it just means you should understand the system before deciding whether the house, neighborhood and educational options work together for your family.

FAQSt. Louis Public Schools FAQ

Is SLPS accredited?

As of early 2026, SLPS is provisionally accredited. The district lost full accreditation in 2007, regained it in 2017, and on January 13, 2026 the Missouri State Board of Education voted to downgrade it to provisional — citing governance and financial issues such as leadership turnover and repeated audit delays, rather than classroom academic scores. Do I have to send my kids to the SLPS school assigned to my address?

Not necessarily. St. Louis City families may have magnet, charter, private and other educational options in addition to the neighborhood-assigned school. Availability and admission are not guaranteed, so verify current eligibility, application deadlines and enrollment procedures before relying on a specific option.

How is the St. Louis city school district different from St. Louis County schools?

The City of St. Louis is an independent city and is not part of St. Louis County. The city has one city-wide public district, St. Louis Public Schools, while St. Louis County contains roughly two dozen separate public school districts (around 23). Can I still buy in South City with school-age kids?

Absolutely, but do the school research before deciding whether a particular home works for your family. Some buyers are comfortable using SLPS magnet, charter or other options, while others prefer a traditional neighborhood-school path and search elsewhere. The right answer depends on your priorities, budget and the specific educational options available to your children.
