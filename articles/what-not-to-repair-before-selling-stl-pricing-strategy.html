5 Expensive St. Louis Home Repairs You Might Not Need Before Selling

Last updated October 9, 2026

Should you replace the roof, update the kitchen, or install a new HVAC system before putting your house on the market? Sometimes. But spending more money doesn’t automatically mean selling for more money.

I’ve walked through plenty of St. Louis homes where the seller was preparing to spend thousands of dollars before listing.

A new water heater. Fresh flooring throughout. A kitchen renovation. Maybe replacing an HVAC system because it’s getting old.

My first question isn’t, “How much will that cost?”

It’s “What changes if we do it?”

Will the improvement increase what buyers are willing to pay? Will it remove something that’s keeping buyers from writing an offer? Will it prevent a problem during inspections? Or are we spending money that the seller might never recover?

Those are different questions, and they don’t always have the same answer.

Here’s how I help sellers think through them.

Before We Talk About Repairs, Let’s Talk About What Your House Is Worth

I generally look at a property from three perspectives.

First: What is the home worth today, in its current condition?

Is it already move-in ready? Does it have some dated finishes but function perfectly well? Or does its condition limit the number of buyers willing to consider it?

I look at comparable sales, current competition, and what buyers are responding to in the market.

Second: What could the property reasonably sell for if it were renovated?

This gives us a potential ceiling. But that ceiling isn’t necessarily the best destination.

If you spend $40,000 improving a house and the market only supports a $25,000 increase in sale price, we’ve created a more attractive house without necessarily creating a better financial outcome for you.

Third: What are similar properties selling for in as-is condition?

This helps establish the lower end of our pricing range. I’m primarily looking at actual as-is comparable sales, not simply subtracting an investor’s estimated repair costs and profit margin from a renovated value.

Together, those three perspectives help us understand the range of possibilities.

Then comes the part that matters most: Where does the seller actually want to land?

Not everyone wants to renovate. Not everyone has the money. Some sellers need to move quickly. Others have time and are willing to make improvements if the numbers justify them.

There’s no reason to make a $30,000 recommendation before understanding any of that.

My Three Questions Before Recommending a Repair

1. Does it increase or preserve value?

Not every repair increases your home’s value.

Sometimes it simply protects the value that’s already there.

An active roof leak, for example, may not be obvious during the first showing. But when the buyer’s inspector discovers it, that can change the negotiation considerably.

Addressing the leak before listing may not add a dollar-for-dollar premium to the sale price. It may help prevent a reduction, repair demand, or failed transaction.

That’s still financially meaningful.

On the other hand, replacing a perfectly functioning water heater simply because it’s old may accomplish very little.

Age and condition are not the same thing.

2. Does it remove a roadblock for buyers?

I think about buyer objections as roadblocks.

Some are visible immediately. Others appear later during inspections.

A room crowded with furniture may feel smaller than it actually is. Worn carpet can make an otherwise maintained home feel tired. Walls that haven’t been painted in ten years may look dull, even though nothing is technically wrong with them.

Those things can affect a buyer’s emotional response.

And sometimes the solution is remarkably simple: remove furniture, declutter, rearrange a room, or paint.

Other roadblocks are less visible. A known defect might not discourage someone during a showing but could become a significant issue once inspections begin.

Removing a roadblock doesn’t always mean repairing something. Sometimes it means improving it, removing it, or pricing the home with that condition in mind.

3. Is there a municipal occupancy requirement?

This is an important distinction in St. Louis.

Depending on the municipality, a property may need an occupancy inspection or other approvals associated with a change in occupancy.

Those requirements aren’t necessarily the same things buyers notice during showings.

An inspector may identify an issue that wouldn’t have stopped a buyer from writing an offer in the first place.

And while we can anticipate common inspection concerns, we can’t know every item a municipal inspector will identify.

That’s why, where an occupancy inspection is required, I recommend ordering it as soon as the property is listed.

That gives us an actual list to work from rather than spending money guessing what might be required.

The municipality’s rules determine what must be corrected, when, and whether any responsibility can legally be transferred.

If we’re selling a home at a move-in-ready market price, the buyer reasonably expects to be able to move in. Getting the required occupancy issues resolved is part of delivering that transaction.

Five Expensive Improvements I Would Question Before Spending the Money

These aren’t five repairs you should never make.

They’re five expenses I would want to evaluate in context before recommending them.

1. Replacing an old but functioning water heater

Let’s say the water heater is getting older, but it’s working properly.

The rest of the home was renovated about ten years ago. It’s clean, maintained, and presents well.

Would replacing the water heater meaningfully change what buyers are willing to pay?

Probably not enough to justify automatically recommending it.

As I explain in my video, if it’s functioning, don’t fix it just because it’s old.

Now change the circumstances. Suppose the water heater is leaking or shows signs of failure. That’s a different conversation.

The recommendation depends on the condition of the component and the context of the house.

2. Installing a new HVAC system

HVAC replacements can be expensive, and buyers certainly care about heating and cooling.

But a system’s age alone doesn’t tell me whether the seller should replace it.

I want to know whether it functions, whether there are known defects, whether it’s been maintained, and how its condition compares with competing homes.

A functioning older system in an otherwise well-maintained property is different from a system that doesn’t work.

There can also be financing, insurance, safety, or inspection considerations that change the decision.

The question isn’t whether a new HVAC system is desirable.

It’s whether installing one before selling produces a better outcome than disclosing the existing condition and pricing appropriately.

3. Renovating a functional kitchen or bathroom

This is where sellers can spend substantial money trying to reach the top of the market.

A dated kitchen may be perfectly functional. Buyers might dislike the cabinets or countertops, but that doesn’t automatically mean the seller should replace them.

I’d compare the home with both current-condition and renovated sales.

How much higher are the renovated properties selling for? What would it actually cost to bring this home to a comparable standard? And how much time would the project add before listing?

Sometimes the difference justifies the work.

Other times, the seller is better off allowing the next owner to make those choices.

And there may be a middle ground: paint, hardware, lighting, or other selective improvements instead of a complete renovation.

4. Replacing windows simply because they’re dated

Older windows can be an objection for buyers, particularly if they’re difficult to operate, damaged, leaking, or showing significant deterioration.

But replacing every window before selling isn’t automatically necessary.

I want to distinguish between a functional older window and a window with a real defect.

If the windows are operating properly and the home is otherwise positioned appropriately for its condition, replacing them may not change the buyer pool enough to justify the expense.

If there are serious condition, safety, or inspection issues, that’s a different situation.

Again, context matters.

5. Replacing an entire roof before understanding its condition

A roof is a significant component of a house, and problems with it can affect inspections, insurance, financing, and negotiations.

But an older roof isn’t necessarily a failed roof.

Before recommending replacement, I’d want to understand its actual condition and any known defects.

Sometimes a specific repair is appropriate. Sometimes replacement is necessary. And sometimes the roof is functioning adequately and its age simply needs to be considered in the property’s market position.

I wouldn’t automatically recommend an expensive replacement based on age alone.

Nor would I ignore a genuine problem just because the seller wants to avoid spending money.

Sometimes the Best Improvements Aren’t Really Repairs

A seller can have a house that’s structurally sound and mechanically functional but still struggles to make a good first impression.

That’s where presentation matters.

Decluttering, moving furniture, staging with what you already own, and selective painting can change how a home feels without a major renovation.

Painting is a good example.

If the walls were painted recently and still look clean, why repaint?

But if the paint is ten years old, scuffed, faded, or dull, fresh paint might be the one improvement that makes the entire home feel cleaner and better maintained.

I generally favor neutral colors when preparing a home for broad market appeal—not because everyone loves neutral colors, but because they tend to create fewer objections than highly personalized choices.

The goal isn’t to erase the home’s personality.

It’s to help buyers see the house instead of getting distracted by things they would immediately want to change.

What If You Don’t Want to Make Repairs?

Then we look at the price.

This is why I establish the current-condition, renovated, and as-is comparable values before making recommendations.

A seller doesn’t have to renovate to the top of that range.

If a property needs improvements and the seller doesn’t want to make them, we can evaluate how that affects its position against competing listings.

Maybe the property still appeals to owner-occupants who are comfortable making improvements.

Maybe its condition puts it closer to the investor market.

Or maybe the seller only needs to make a few inexpensive presentation changes to compete effectively.

Price is a lever. Repairs are another lever. We don’t have to pull both all the way.

What we shouldn’t do is assume that money spent on improvements automatically comes back at closing.

Selling As-Is Doesn’t Mean You Should Do Nothing

There’s a difference between selling a home in its existing condition and presenting it carelessly.

You might decide not to replace the kitchen, HVAC system, or flooring.

That doesn’t mean you should leave every room cluttered or ignore inexpensive ways to make the home more appealing.

An as-is selling strategy can still include cleaning, staging, selective painting, yard maintenance, and other presentation improvements.

It also doesn’t eliminate disclosure obligations, municipal requirements, or the need to understand what the contract requires.

The right strategy is based on the property’s condition, the seller’s goals, and the market—not the words as-is alone.

Don’t Forget the Repairs That Show Up After the Offer

A buyer’s first impression is only part of the transaction.

The inspection period can introduce issues that weren’t visible during the showing.

That’s why I consider known defects when developing the selling strategy, even when buyers aren’t likely to notice them immediately.

A repair may not increase the sale price, but addressing it could preserve the agreed price or reduce the chance of an inspection dispute.

That doesn’t mean every inspection recommendation needs to be completed before listing.

Inspectors identify conditions and concerns. Buyers decide what matters to them. Sellers evaluate the cost and contractual implications.

I want to understand those risks before we list, not pretend they don’t exist.

And when municipal occupancy requirements apply, I want that inspection ordered early enough to avoid unnecessary surprises later.

So How Do You Decide What to Fix?

Here’s the process I use when evaluating a St. Louis home before selling:

QuestionWhat we’re trying to learn
What will the home sell for today?Its current market position
What could it sell for renovated?The potential upside
What are comparable as-is homes selling for?The lower end of the market range
Which improvements change buyer perception?Visible roadblocks worth removing
Which known defects could affect negotiations?Value we may need to protect
What does the municipality require?Compliance and occupancy obligations
What does the seller want to accomplish?The strategy that makes sense for their situation

From there, we decide what to repair, improve, remove, leave alone, or account for through price.

There isn’t a universal checklist that makes those decisions for every house.

A repair that makes sense for one property might be unnecessary for another house just down the street.

  • I have a list of repairs and don’t know which are worth doing
  • I’m deciding whether to fix something or price for it
  • I have a cash offer for the house as‑is
  • I’m under contract and the buyer sent a repair request

My Advice: Don’t Spend the Money Until You Understand the Alternatives

Before you spend $10,000 replacing something, I’d want to show you what comparable homes are selling for with and without that improvement.

Before you renovate a kitchen, I’d want to understand whether the renovated sales support the investment.

And before you decide to sell entirely as-is, I’d want to identify the inexpensive changes that could remove buyer objections without taking on a major project.

You don’t need to fix everything before selling. You need to understand what fixing—or not fixing—actually changes.

That’s the conversation I’d rather have before you spend the money.

If you’re trying to decide what to repair, what to leave alone, and how to price your property, start with the house you actually have—not a generic list of improvements.

I’ll help you compare its current condition with renovated and as-is sales, identify meaningful buyer roadblocks, and understand applicable occupancy requirements. Then we can decide what makes financial sense for you.
