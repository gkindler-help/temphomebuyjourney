Moving to St. Louis

Moving From Houston to St. Louis: Find the Area With the House You’re Leaving

Last updated October 30, 2026

Houston buyers usually ask two things about St. Louis: where is the house like mine, and why does everything here seem older? This page answers both by house type, with 2026 St. Louis sales next to Census figures for the Houston suburbs. One difference runs through all of it: Houston builds on slabs, and St. Louis builds a lot of its houses on basements.

Moving From Houston, Answered

Which part of St. Louis is most like the Houston suburbs?

Match the decade your house was built. Katy, Cypress and Pearland homes from the 2000s line up with Jefferson County’s newer towns; 1990s Sugar Land and Woodlands houses with Wildwood; Heights bungalows with Maplewood and Webster Groves; and Pasadena’s 1970s ranches with South County. House type by house type ↓

Does St. Louis have new subdivisions like Katy and Cypress?

A few, mostly south in Jefferson County. Katy’s and Pearland’s median houses date from 2003 and the Cypress area’s from 2007; in 2026 St. Louis-area resales, Herculaneum’s median was 2009, Hillsboro’s 2002 and the Jefferson County side of Eureka’s 2021. Jefferson County’s newer towns ↓

Where should I look in St. Louis if I’m leaving Sugar Land or The Woodlands?

Wildwood. Sugar Land’s median house dates from 1994 and The Woodlands’ from 2000; Wildwood’s 2026 sales had a 1992 median and 3,342 sq ft above ground. Chesterfield offers similar size from about ten years earlier. Sugar Land and Woodlands matches ↓

What part of St. Louis is like the Houston Heights?

Maplewood, Webster Groves and the South City neighborhoods, minus the rebuilds. Their 2026 medians were built in 1929 and 1936, and South City’s brick bungalows and flats date from the 1910s to the 1940s, while a quarter of the Heights went up in the 2010s. Heights and Montrose matches ↓

How much more room does a St. Louis basement give a Houston buyer?

Up to a whole floor. A Houston house is all above ground, because the water table sits too close to the surface for basements. Many St. Louis houses add a level underneath the listed square footage, for storage, a rec room or a storm shelter. Slab vs. basement ↓

Leaving a newer Katy, Cypress or Pearland home?

Head south of St. Louis County. Katy and Pearland both have 2003 medians, and the Cypress area is newer at 2007, according to Census data; St. Louis County’s houses mostly predate 1980. Jefferson County, across the Meramec River, is where the region’s newer resale subdivisions are.

Leaving Sugar Land or The Woodlands?

Leaving the Heights or Montrose?

You’ll find older originals here, not rebuilds. The Heights pairs a pre-war core with a construction boom: 16.7% of its housing predates 1940, and 26.6% went up in the 2010s, according to Census data. St. Louis’s walkable inner suburbs kept their original houses.

Leaving a 1950s–70s Pasadena ranch?

Go to South County. Pasadena’s housing centers on the 1970s, with a median of 1975 or 1977 depending on the Census measure, and roughly a third built before 1970. South St. Louis County was built out in the same decades, and ranches are its most common style.

Leaving an inner-loop condo, River Oaks or Memorial?

For condo living, the Central West End is the Montrose of St. Louis: Forest Park on one side, the hospitals on the other, and blocks of older apartment buildings converted to condos in between. Clayton trades that for an office district with condo towers.

For River Oaks or Memorial, the counterparts are Ladue and Clayton. Ladue’s 2026 median was $1,430,000 for about 4,400 sq ft built around 1955; Clayton’s was $1,240,000 for about 3,600 sq ft from 1936. Location carries most of the price in both. Ladue, Clayton and their neighbors →

Slab vs. basement: what does a Houston buyer gain?

Floor space you can’t get in Houston. Houston’s high water table and flat, flood-prone ground keep almost every house on a slab, so the square footage on the listing is the whole house. Here, a lot of the ranches and two-stories in the tables above have a full basement under the main floor, and the listed square footage counts only the floors above ground.

A Pasadena ranch buyer looking at a 1,766 sq ft Mehlville ranch, the 2026 median there, may be looking at nearly double the floor area once the basement is counted: storage, a laundry room, a workshop, a family room if it’s finished, and a safe place when the tornado sirens go off. What changes when you move from Houston to St. Louis?

Mostly the paperwork around the house. Houston buyers usually notice these five:

  • An income tax. Missouri has one and Texas doesn’t, and a job inside St. Louis City adds a 1% earnings tax wherever you live. How the earnings tax works →
  • A different set of tax lines. In place of the MUD line on many Houston bills, St. Louis bills carry their own layers, such as fire and library districts. Judge each house by its actual bill. Tax bills by school district →
  • Zoning by city. Houston leans on deed restrictions. Here, each of St. Louis County’s 88 municipalities zones its own land, and the County zones the unincorporated areas. Which government a house falls under →
  • An inspection to sell. Many County cities won’t let a house change hands without an occupancy inspection. Which cities require one →
  • Water below, not around. Flood zones were the Houston worry; here, check the basement walls and the sewer line. I recommend a $150 to $300 sewer scope on every St. Louis house.

Yes. Houston is a long day’s drive away, so plan on touring by live video, keeping the inspection contingency and closing remotely through the title company if you can’t make the trip. How buying from out of state works →

Do St. Louis houses flood like Houston houses?

The usual worry here is different: water getting into a basement more than a flooded street. Some areas along the Meramec and Missouri rivers do flood, so check the flood map for any address, then look at the basement walls and floor and ask about past water.
