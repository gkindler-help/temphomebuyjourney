Making an Offer

How to Get a Deal on a House in St. Louis: Start With the Houses Everyone Skipped

Last updated October 30, 2026

The deals in St. Louis aren’t hidden. They’re the houses that have been sitting. In 2026, about one County sale in three sat more than 30 days, and three out of four of those closed under their original asking price, by a median of $13,075. Most buyers scroll past them. I tell my buyers to look at them first.

Getting a Deal, Answered

Where are the deals on houses in St. Louis?

In the houses that have been sitting. Of 2,864 St. Louis County sales in 2026, the 898 that sat 30+ days closed at a median 95.2% of their original list price, and the 630 that had a price cut closed at 91.6%. Houses that sold within 30 days got 101.7%. See the 2026 sales →

Should I look at houses that have been on the market a while?

Yes. I tell every buyer to. A house that sat isn’t automatically a problem; often it was priced too high or marketed badly. Find out why it sat before you decide what it’s worth. Why buyers skip them →

How do I find houses that have been sitting?

Look at cumulative days on market (CDOM), not days on market. When a house is pulled and relisted, listing sites can restart the count at zero. The MLS keeps the full count, and I can pull it for any area and budget. How to find them →

Will a seller take less than asking on an overpriced house or one that’s been sitting?

Often, but you find out before you write the offer, not after. On select houses, ones that are clearly overpriced, have been sitting, or both, I call the listing agent, share my buyer’s budget and ask whether an offer in that range makes sense for the seller. In those cases, the price we land on is typically below market. What I ask the listing agent →

Do as-is houses sell for less in St. Louis?

Yes. In 2026, as-is sales closed at a median 82.2% of the price per square foot of comparable standard sales in the same school district. Part of that gap is the cost of repairs, so an inspection decides whether it’s a deal. The as-is numbers →

At what price is there the most room to negotiate?

Under $150,000 the gap is widest in percentage terms: houses that sat closed at 85.9% of original list. Above $750,000 it’s widest in dollars, a median $75,000 under original list. By price range →

Is a seller credit better than a lower price?

Usually, if you use it to buy down your rate or cover cash you don’t have. A price cut lowers your payment a little and your down payment slightly. A credit of the same size, spent on discount points, typically lowers your payment more. Run your numbers in the calculator. Price cut vs. credit calculator →

Why do most buyers skip the houses that have been sitting?

Because new listings get the attention, and a house that’s been listed for two months looks like something’s wrong with it. Sometimes something is. More often it was priced over the comps, photographed badly or listed at the wrong time of year, and the seller has been carrying the taxes, insurance and utilities, and often a mortgage, every month since.

I personally tell my buyers to look at the houses that have been sitting. They’re the ones where the seller is ready to talk, and where you aren’t competing with five other offers in the first weekend.

From one of my sales: Expired at $180,000, then sold for $200,000. A sitting house isn’t always a discount: this split-level expired in the fall with another agent, came back in the spring with new photos and no new work, and sold for $20,000 more. How do you find the houses that have been sitting?

Look at cumulative days on market, which the MLS calls CDOM. Days on market restarts when a listing is pulled and put back up, and listing sites can show that fresh count. CDOM keeps counting through relists, so a house that looks 6 days old online can be 120 days into trying to sell.

Price history helps too. A house that’s had a price cut has already told you the seller is moving: in 2026, County houses with a cut closed at a median 91.6% of their original price. Whether that cut is an opening or a warning depends on why it was needed. Is a price cut a buying opportunity? →

What happens when I call the listing agent about an overpriced or sitting house?

I don’t make this call on every house. I make it on select houses: ones that are clearly overpriced against the comps, ones that have been sitting too long, or both. On those, I call the listing agent before we write anything. I share my buyer’s budget and ask whether an offer in that range makes sense for the seller. In these cases the number we land on is typically below market.

The call does two things. It tells the listing agent there’s a real buyer with a real budget, which matters to a seller who’s been carrying the house for months. And it tells us, before you spend money on an inspection, whether the seller is ready to deal or still holding out for the original price.

It’s also where I learn what the seller actually needs: a quick close, more time to move, help with something on their end. That’s often worth as much as the price, and it rarely shows up in the listing. What else I ask the listing agent →

How much room is there at your price?

The share of houses that sit, and how far they come down, changes with price. Under $150,000, nearly half the houses sat 30+ days and those closed at a median 85.9% of original list. Do as-is houses sell for less?

Yes, but not against their own asking price. As-is listings closed at a median 97.1% of original list in 2026, close to standard sales. The discount is already built into the price. Measured against comparable standard sales in the same school district, within 25% of the size and 15 years of the age, as-is sales closed at a median 82.2% of the price per square foot.

Is a house that’s back on the market a deal?

It can be. A house that fell out of contract has a seller who already thought they were done, and the second time around they’re usually more realistic. Find out why the first deal fell apart: a buyer’s financing is the seller’s bad luck, and an inspection finding is your next inspection’s first item.

Is a seller credit worth more than a lower price?

Usually, if you’re financing. Taking $9,000 off a $300,000 price lowers your loan by $9,000 and your payment by about $57 a month at 7% with 5% down. The same $9,000 as a seller credit spent on discount points can lower your rate by roughly three-quarters of a point, about $149 a month on the same loan if each point is worth 0.25%, or it can cover closing costs you’d otherwise pay in cash. The seller nets the same either way.

Price cut or seller credit: what does each do to your payment?

Principal and interest on a 30-year loan only; taxes, insurance and mortgage insurance aren’t included. One discount point costs 1% of the loan, and how much it lowers your rate changes daily, so replace 0.25 with your lender’s quote. Credit limits used for the warning: conventional 3% of the price with under 10% down, 6% with 10–25% down, 9% with more than 25% down; FHA 6%; VA 4% in concessions, with normal closing costs allowed on top.

Credits have limits by loan type, and the credit can’t go toward your down payment. If the house appraises low, a price cut also matters more than a credit. What seller-paid closing costs can cover →

When is a cheap house a bad deal?

When the discount is smaller than the problem. A house that sat because of a failing sewer lateral, foundation movement or water in the basement can cost more to fix than the price came down. Get the inspection and a sewer scope on every St. Louis house, and price the big items before you call it a deal. What buyers regret about cheap houses →
