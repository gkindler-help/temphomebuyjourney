The Repairs Didn’t Pass. It Cost the Seller $16,820.

Last updated October 28, 2026

Not every lesson is a win. We had 7441 Maple Avenue in Maplewood under contract at $270,000. At the final walk-through, the buyers’ inspector said most of the repair items weren’t resolved, and the buyers wanted $16,820 off or out. The house closed, at their price. Step into my seat, make the calls, then read how each one played out.

What was wrong before the buyers ever saw it?

In my view, the renovation. This was an investor-owned house, and the contractor hadn’t delivered the work promised in the seller’s first renovation. What got done was cosmetic and poor, not a proper, complete renovation.

That matters because it came before any buyer, inspection or repair agreement. A later repair list doesn’t erase poor original workmanship. It sits underneath everything that follows, and in this sale it came back at the final walk-through.

What did 20 showings and mixed feedback tell us?

That the price needed attention before the activity faded. We advertised around $275,000, and by August 6, 2024 we’d had about 20 showings with mixed opinions on price.

I recommended a possible $5,000 adjustment to keep buyers coming through, rather than simply defending the asking price. When to lower your price →

How did a $265,000 offer become $270,000?

We countered. On August 11 the buyers offered $265,000 with $3,000 earnest money, a 12-day inspection period, an appraisal rider and a home warranty, and not contingent on financing. I recommended countering at $270,000 with a 10-day inspection period. The buyers accepted on August 12, with closing set for September 12.

“Not contingent on financing” didn’t mean cash. It meant the contract didn’t give the buyers a financing exit, and the final settlement still showed a loan.

What happened at the first inspection?

The buyers sent an inspection notice on August 19, and the remaining repair work came to about $6,000 for the seller’s contractor to complete, as we understood it then. On August 28 we proposed a $3,000 closing credit, and on August 29 the inspection was reported resolved.

That’s where most sales would be done negotiating. This one wasn’t, because the agreed work still had to be done, done well and documented.

Why ask the contractor for itemized invoices and lien waivers?

Because a bill that doesn’t say what was fixed doesn’t prove anything. On September 20, I asked for paid invoices and lien waivers, and followed up because the bills submitted didn’t specify which repair items had been completed.

That gap became the whole argument at the walk-through. If you can’t show which agreed items were done, and done properly, the buyer’s inspector’s report becomes the only record in the room.

What happened at the final walk-through?

The buyers’ reinspection on September 20 listed 4 items resolved and 26 not resolved, and the buyers asked to lower the price from $270,000 to $253,180, a $16,820 cut.

Those 26 are the inspector’s status counts, not a verified count of repairs the seller had promised. They included the pitch of the front porch roof, shingles on a bay roof the inspector considered flat, soffit and fascia deterioration, disputed tuckpointing, a loose handrail, and basement water and sump issues. The buyers also got a repair quote of $18,350:

The seller’s contractor saw it more narrowly: a different reason for how the bay roof was finished, a cleared drain plus a threshold and sump pump already added, a leaking PVC connection and the handrail it agreed to fix, the original missing mortar addressed rather than the whole wall, and soffit and fascia outside its original scope. Two sides, two reports.

Could the buyers just walk away?

Not the way I understood this contract. The buyers sent a mutual release, and the seller wouldn’t sign it as presented. As I recall it, I told their agent by phone that their contingencies had passed and that ending the contract over the final walk-through wasn’t an option under this contract.

That’s my reading of this contract, not a rule for every Missouri sale. Whether a party can end a contract, delay closing or seek other remedies depends on the signed contract, the deadlines and what each side actually performed. What choices did the seller have?

Three, and I laid them out for him on September 21:

  1. Accept the reduction to $253,180 and close.
  2. Refuse it, and get legal advice if the closing failed.
  3. Counter, and give his contractor another chance to correct the work.

We first tried the third path: I told the buyers’ side the seller wouldn’t sign the release as presented, proposed letting the contractor correct the shortcomings, and both sides signed an extension of closing to September 27. My job was to make sure the seller understood the tradeoff in each choice and made it knowingly. It wasn’t my job to decide it for him.

Why did water in the basement change the math?

Because it stopped being an argument about workmanship. On September 23, after a rain, the contractors found water in the basement. That’s a condition no one could talk around.

On September 25, the seller chose to accept $253,180, and the price amendment was signed by both sides.

How did it finally close?

On October 1, 2024, at $253,180. Closing moved from September 12 to September 27 to October 1, with further amendments along the way, and the seller signed remotely. The title company confirmed it closed and funded on October 1.

The final settlement statement shows the $253,180 price and no separate seller credit; the $3,000 on it is the buyers’ earnest money.

Did the seller come out ahead?

No, not against the contract. The buyers got the price they asked for. What the seller got was a closed sale instead of a failed one, with a house that had taken on water.

I wasn’t pleased with how this one went, and I’d rather show it than hide it. Most of what went wrong was settled before the house ever listed.

What should sellers take from this sale?

Get the renovation right before you list, document every repair, know the difference between a walk-through objection and a way out of the contract, and decide with your eyes open.

Can a cosmetic renovation hurt you before you have a buyer?

Yes. If the original work wasn’t done properly, a buyer’s inspection will find it, and later repairs on top of poor work can reopen the fight at the final walk-through.

What proves a repair was done?

Itemized, paid invoices that name the work, lien waivers, photos and permits where needed. “Complete” on a contractor’s bill isn’t the same as “resolved” on an inspector’s report.

Is a failed walk-through the same as a right to cancel?

Not necessarily. What a buyer can do after contingencies pass depends on the contract and what each side performed. Get a lawyer’s advice on that question, not just your agent’s.

When does taking a price reduction make sense?

When the cost and risk of fighting are worse than the cut. Once the basement took on water, the seller chose a certain closing at $253,180 over an uncertain dispute at $270,000.

Ask Me About the Repair Request

Questions This Sale Helps Answer

Can a buyer ask for more after the inspection is resolved?

They can object at the final walk-through if agreed repairs weren’t done or weren’t done properly. What happens next depends on the contract. Here it ended in a negotiated price reduction.

What should a seller ask their contractor for before closing?

Paid, itemized invoices that list each repair item, signed lien waivers and a short written explanation of what was done. On this sale, bills that didn’t say which items were finished left the buyers’ reinspection as the only detailed record.

Does “not contingent on financing” mean a cash buyer?

No. It means the contract didn’t include a financing contingency. These buyers still used a loan.
