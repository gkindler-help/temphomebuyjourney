Why did Roy and Karen leave a 44-year home for Fenton?

To be close to their grandkids, in a house that fit the life they live now. Their kids had moved to Fenton, and after 44 years the house in South City no longer matched their routine.

Roy and Karen bought their home in the Carondelet area of South St. Louis before they had kids: a 100-plus-year-old two-story just north of Bates. They raised their kids there and stayed 44 years. Over time, the anchors changed. Their kids moved to Fenton, their longtime church closed, and their routine changed while their house didn’t.

So they weren’t downsizing. They were right-sizing. The list was short but important: be close to their grandkids, room for Karen’s music and crafts, a garage where Roy could work on his car, green space out back, and one-level living for the years ahead. I wrote about how they figured that out in Where Should I Move When Downsizing in St. Louis? This is how the purchase actually played out.

Did they need a certain school district or the City of Fenton?

No. Their goal was the location and the house, so they didn’t need a particular school district or to be inside the City of Fenton. That opened up the Jefferson County side.

Before San Luis, Roy and Karen wrote offers on two other homes in Fenton: one on the Lindbergh side, which sold for about $40,000 over asking, and one on the Parkway side. Neither worked out.

A lot of this site is about school districts, because that’s how St. Louis sorts its housing data and how many buyers search. But my focus is always the client’s goals. For Roy and Karen, this was about the location and the house, not the school district, and they didn’t need to be inside the City of Fenton to hit their goal. So I encouraged them to focus on the home.

That opened up the Jefferson County side of Fenton, south of the Meramec. San Luis Drive is unincorporated Jefferson County in the Northwest R-I district, and it’s about 1.5 miles, roughly five minutes by car, from their grandson. On location, it didn’t just meet the goal. It beat it.

How fast can a buyer tour a new listing and write an offer?

The same day. Roy and Karen had an offer ready to sign about an hour and a half after I sent them the house.

The house was listed on a Sunday morning. For a seller, that’s absolutely the worst time to list. By Sunday, most buyers have already locked into an offer or have their showing schedule set for the weekend.

We saw it as an opportunity. This is also the honest answer to “how fast can you be available?” I sent them the house. They said yes within five minutes, and I said, let’s meet there in 45.

Should I walk away if the seller won’t pay my agent’s commission?

Not automatically. Look at your net number first. Roy and Karen paid the commission themselves and were still all in at $323,000, $27,000 under their $350,000 budget, on the house that met their goals.

Here’s the bad news: it was an estate sale, and you can’t control all the variables. Only the ones you can.

While we were the only offer on the table, the sellers countered us on commission. They didn’t want to pay buyer-agent compensation. A lesson for sellers: that is terrible advice. My buyers were ready to walk. You had the only buyer in hand, on a Sunday listing, and you gave them a reason to leave.

They didn’t leave. We looked at the number that matters, the net. On Tuesday, they presented a modified offer: they would now pay the commission themselves. It’s certainly rare for my buyers to pay it. But the net still worked, on a house that beat their goals on location and the house itself.

Do I have to raise my offer when the seller asks for highest and best?

No. Highest and best asks every buyer for their final offer; it doesn’t require you to go up. If you know where you stand, you can stand on your offer. Roy and Karen kept the same offer they made on Tuesday, and the sellers accepted it.

By Wednesday, once my clients had agreed to pay the commission, the sellers received other offers. Now it was a multiple-offer situation. Instead of negotiating with us directly, the sellers called for highest and best. Roy and Karen were still in the game with the same offer they’d presented on Tuesday.

Here’s what I want to point out: the most motivated buyer in that pile was my clients. They showed up the day the house was listed, on a Sunday, when most buyers had already locked into an offer or set their showing schedule. It’s not about the first offer; it’s about the motivated buyer. My buyers weren’t happy that the sellers called for highest and best instead of working with them, and I understand why. In the end, they were the highest and best.

We were always the best offer. Inexperienced agents and buyers could have walked away emotionally at this point, and I told my buyers they could walk if they wanted to. In my view, the sellers were making a mistake by not taking their offer. But based on my conversations with the listing agent, I knew we would have the highest offer in the multiple-offer situation. Building solid rapport with the other agent matters here. Either she didn’t tell the other agents that her sellers wouldn’t pay commission, or she told them and they didn’t raise their price.

Of course, we could have pushed back and put the commission back in. But there are two sides to this. On one hand, the sellers could have run my buyers off emotionally. On the other, my buyers had goals to meet. So I asked them: if we removed how the sellers responded to your offer, does that change the house?

I know we’re in a people business. But a month from now, the sellers’ response to your offer won’t matter. What will matter is hitting your goals and being happy in your home. Roy and Karen stood on their offer, and the sellers accepted it. The house sold at its $315,000 list price after four days on the market.

And for anyone wondering whether standing on that offer meant overpaying: the house appraised above the contract price.

What should you ask for when the inspection finds a failing furnace?

Ask for the repair before closing, and a home warranty for what comes later. That’s what we asked for here, and we got both.

The inspection turned up several minor issues, and then we found a furnace that was failing. A minor item is something you can live with. A failing furnace going into a St. Louis fall isn’t. I recommended we ask the sellers to repair the furnace and provide a home warranty. We got both.

Will a home warranty cover a problem the inspector already found?

No. The two asks do different jobs. A home warranty doesn’t cover a known pre-existing condition, and once the inspector found the furnace failing, it was known. So the furnace had to be fixed by the sellers before closing. The warranty covers what nobody could see yet.

What their Ultimate home warranty covers
  • Term: 365 days, starting at closing.
  • Cost per claim: a $75 service call fee, paid to the technician, whatever the repair costs.
  • Systems: heating, air conditioning and ductwork, electrical, plumbing and stoppages, the water heater and a permanently installed sump pump.
  • Appliances and more: dishwasher, oven and range, built-in microwave, garbage disposal, garage door opener, attic, ceiling and exhaust fans, and re-keying up to six locks.
  • Ultimate adds: the clothes washer, dryer and kitchen refrigerator; toilets (up to $600 each), faucets, shower valves and exterior hose bibs; slab leaks (up to $500); smoke detectors and exterior outlets; garage door springs, hinges and remotes; and the smaller heating and cooling parts the base plan excludes, such as condensate pumps, drain pans and refrigerant recapture.
  • Extras that matter on a repair: up to $1,000 a year for roof leaks over the living area, as long as the roof doesn’t need replacing; up to $250 per occurrence for permits; up to $250 to correct code violations; up to $250 for an earlier improper installation or mismatched equipment; haul-away of a replaced item; and coverage of a required upgrade when matching heating and cooling equipment has to be replaced.
  • Limits: up to $5,000 per 12 months for each heating system, air conditioning system and ductwork, and generally $5,000 per covered item.
  • Hidden problems: buyer coverage includes pre-existing conditions that couldn’t have been found by a visual inspection or a simple mechanical test. Known conditions, misuse and abuse aren’t covered.
  • Repeat failures: if a covered repair fails within 30 days, it’s fixed again without another service fee.
Using a home warrantyCall the warranty company as soon as something stops working, before you hire anyone. It picks the contractor and won’t reimburse work done without its approval. Also, the plan renews automatically at the end of the year unless you cancel, so decide before then whether to keep it. Coverage details come from Choice Home Warranty’s 2026 real estate buyer terms. In that brochure, a one-year Ultimate buyer plan for a single-family home was $660, compared with $450 for the base plan. Read your own contract for its exact plan and limits.

Does a Ring doorbell stay with the house when it sells?

Usually, yes. Anything affixed to the house that isn’t excluded in the contract goes with the house, and a mounted video doorbell or spotlight is affixed. A seller who wants to keep one should exclude it up front.

We thought the drama was done. Then, the evening before closing, the listing agent texted me: the sellers were taking the Ring doorbell and the spotlight.

Buyers and sellers both need to know this one. Anything affixed to the house that isn’t specifically excluded in the contract goes with the house. I told her they’re fixtures. We’d had a month to talk about a replacement, or they could have been excluded in the agent remarks, something I’ve done many times on my own listings. That didn’t happen, so they were now part of my buyers’ real property.

Removing them would also be a material change in the condition of the house, and that can trigger the walk-through notice. My buyers would have been well within their rights to have the sellers cure it before closing.

I believe the agent had a productive conversation with her sellers. The Ring doorbell and the spotlight stayed, and we had a smooth closing.

For sellersIf there’s something attached to the house you want to take with you, such as a doorbell camera, a mounted TV bracket, a light fixture or a curtain rod, exclude it in the contract or the listing remarks before you’re under contract, or swap it out before you list. Night-before-closing surprises put the closing at risk.

What should buyers and sellers take from this sale?

Buyers: keep your goals ahead of the sellers’ behavior. Sellers: don’t give a ready buyer a reason to leave.

Should the way the sellers act change whether you buy the house?

Usually not. Roy and Karen could have walked twice, once over the commission counter and once over highest and best, and both times they’d have walked away from the house that met their goals. A seller’s negotiating style is temporary. The house, the location and the payment are what you live with. Ask the same question I asked them: does how they responded change the house?

Should you start your search with a school district or with your goals?

With your goals. Their goal was to be close to their grandkids in a house that fit their next chapter. That pointed them past the city limits and into unincorporated Jefferson County, and it kept them $27,000 under budget, commission included. Know what your search is really about before you let a boundary decide it for you.

Should a seller fight their only buyer over commission?

No. The sellers here got a list-price offer from a buyer who could move the same day, then made them work for it twice. It nearly cost them their best offer, and calling for highest and best instead of negotiating with the buyer who showed up first didn’t get them a better one. Look at your net, and don’t give a ready buyer a reason to leave. I don’t recommend introducing emotional roadblocks any more than physical ones in the property.

When should a seller decide which fixtures stay?

Before you list. If you want to keep a fixture, exclude it up front. Waiting until the night before closing turns a doorbell into a closing risk.
