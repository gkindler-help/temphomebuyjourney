Who's Actually Representing You?

Do You Actually Need a Buyer's Agent in St. Louis?

Last updated October 8, 2026

Can you skip the buyer’s agent, go straight to the listing agent and save money? You can buy without your own agent—plenty of people do. Whether you save anything is a separate question, and so is what you give up. Here’s how it actually works in St. Louis, from someone who has been on every side of it.

George Kindler· Licensed Missouri Realtor· The Closing Pros LLC· For Buyers

If I Go Straight to the Listing Agent, Will I Save Money?

Maybe—but not automatically. You can call the listing agent, see the house and buy it without hiring your own agent. Three things are true at the same time:

  • Calling the listing agent doesn’t make them your agent. Unless you both agree to something different, they work for the seller, and you’re an unrepresented buyer—what Missouri law calls a customer.
  • Dual agency is an option, with disclosure and consent. If the listing agent offers to represent you too, Missouri allows it only with the consent of both you and the seller, and the agent then works within real limits for both of you.
  • Any savings has to be negotiated. What the seller pays the listing brokerage is set by the listing agreement. Leaving a buyer’s agent out of the deal doesn’t automatically come off your price. A lower price, a credit or a reduced fee comes from negotiation, not from skipping a step.

So the better question is: what am I giving up, and is that worth it for this house? Some buyers—experienced investors, people buying from family, buyers who know the house and the contract well—reasonably decide to go it alone. Others find out halfway through that nobody was watching out for them. The point of this article is to know which one you are before you start.

Three Ways to Buy the Same House

1. Your own buyer’s agent

The agent works for you. In Missouri, you sign a written buyer agency agreement before they start providing brokerage services to you as your agent, and that agreement spells out how they’re paid. They advise you on price, terms, inspections and deadlines, and they keep your negotiating position to themselves.

2. Disclosed dual agency

One agent represents both you and the seller, with everyone’s consent. Under Missouri law, that agent can’t tell the seller you’d pay more than you offered, can’t tell you the seller would take less than the asking price, and can’t share either side’s motivation or confidential information without that person’s consent. They can still guide both of you through the process. What they can’t do is push your number against the seller’s.

3. Unrepresented, working with the seller’s agent

The listing agent represents the seller. Under Missouri law they still have to disclose adverse material facts they know about—or should know about—and if they’re a REALTOR®, the NAR Code of Ethics requires them to treat you honestly. But they don’t advise you, and they have no duty to inspect the property or investigate on your behalf.

What Happens When You Call Me About My Listing

Here’s what I do when a buyer without an agent calls about one of my listings. Some of it is the law; some of it is just how I run my business.

  1. I ask whether you already have an agent.
  2. I explain your representation options—your own agent, me representing both you and the seller with everyone’s consent, or staying unrepresented. Missouri also requires me to give you the state’s Broker Disclosure Form early in our conversation, so you can see those choices in writing.
  3. I find out which one you want. There’s no wrong answer, but I need to know before I do anything else.
  4. I explain what’s required for the showing and any agreement. If you stay unrepresented, I can show you the house as the seller’s agent. If you want me to represent you as well, that’s dual agency, and it has to be agreed to before I start working for you as your agent.

If You’re Unrepresented and Want to Make an Offer

I’ll prepare it. My job at that point is to translate your instructions into the contract—the price you choose, the earnest money you choose, the terms you choose.

Because I work for the seller, here’s what I won’t do:

  • Recommend your offer price or earnest-money amount.
  • Suggest ways to make your offer more competitive.
  • Coach you on negotiating terms or predict how the seller will counter.

During inspections

I’ll explain the notice options the contract gives you, ask which one you choose and write down the terms you request. I won’t recommend what to ask for, whether to ask for repairs or a credit, or how to negotiate it.

For the rest of the transaction

I don’t manage your contract deadlines or remind you when contingencies expire. Those dates are yours to track. My duties stay with the seller—while I still owe you honesty and disclosure of adverse material facts.

Preparing an offer isn’t representing you I can put your decisions on paper accurately. I can’t make them for you, and I can’t tell you whether they’re good ones.

When I Represented Both Sides

I recently represented both the buyer and the seller on the same sale. Before we went forward, I explained what that meant and what I couldn’t do for either of them, and both agreed to it.

From there, I kept each side’s negotiating information to myself. I never told the seller what the buyer might pay, or the buyer what the seller might accept. I still gave both of them professional guidance—how the process works, what the contract says, what their options were. What I didn’t do was argue one side’s price against the other.

Buyers usually ask about the commission next. There was no automatic discount just because one agent was on both sides. Compensation comes from the agreements, and like everything else in the deal, it’s negotiable—but it doesn’t shrink on its own.

Dual agency isn’t a scandal, and it isn’t a shortcut. It’s a narrower kind of help. Some buyers are comfortable with that, especially when the price and terms are straightforward. If you want someone pushing your number against the seller’s, you want your own agent.

Representation Isn’t a Guarantee: An Earnest-Money Story

I represented the seller on this one. The buyer had their own agent.

The buyer waived their financing contingency. A significant part of their down payment depended on selling another home. That sale was delayed, and their financing failed.

My seller offered alternative seller-financing terms to keep the deal together. The buyer turned them down. Their agent walked them through the options available. In the end, the contract terminated and the buyer forfeited their earnest money—on top of what they had already paid for inspections and an appraisal. The house went back on the market.

The buyer’s agent explained the choices. But the choices—waiving the financing contingency, relying on another sale for the down payment, turning down the seller’s terms—were the buyer’s to make. That’s the lesson: representation gets you professional advice and someone advocating for you. It doesn’t take the obligations in the contract off your shoulders, and it can’t guarantee you won’t lose money.

What Representation Actually Means

When you hire a buyer’s agent in Missouri, state law spells out what they owe you: to promote your interests with good faith and loyalty, keep your confidential information confidential, disclose adverse material facts they know or should know about, and account for any money or property they handle for you.

That is what representation is supposed to mean. In practice, it looks different depending on who is actually sitting across from you.

The Distinction That Matters A listing agent represents the seller. Their job is to get the highest price and best terms for the person who hired them. When you call the number on a yard sign, you’re talking to someone whose obligations run to the other side of your transaction. That is not representation. That is access. What a Buyer's Agent in St. Louis Costs

In many St. Louis transactions, the seller still pays buyer-agent compensation out of the sale proceeds. But it’s negotiated now, not automatic. If the seller doesn’t pay it, your written buyer agreement determines what you owe.

The August 2024 NAR settlement changed the mechanics. If you work with a buyer’s agent, NAR rules require a written agreement before they tour a home with you, and that agreement has to spell out their compensation. Missouri law goes further: since August 28, 2025, a buyer agency agreement must be signed before an agent provides brokerage services to you as your agent.

The practical effect: you need to have the compensation conversation with your agent before you walk through the first door. Any agent who is unwilling to have that conversation clearly is not someone you want representing you.

What Good Representation Actually Gets You

A buyer's agent who is doing their job correctly is doing things you cannot see and would not know to ask for. Before a showing, they have pulled the price history, tax record, permit history, and comparable sales. They know whether the price is defensible or aspirational. During a showing, their eyes are on the HVAC age, the basement walls, the condition of the mortar on the exterior, the electrical panel -- not the staged kitchen.

When it is time to write an offer, they know the difference between a market offer and one that overpays. They know which contingencies protect you and which ones you can safely waive in this specific situation. They know how to write inspection objections that produce results rather than antagonizing a seller into termination. They know what the net sheet looks like before you sign anything.

That is what 13 years in the St. Louis market produces. Not a different license -- a different level of preparation and local knowledge.

What Bad Representation Actually Costs You

The agent who calls you within two minutes of clicking a Zillow listing did not know that home existed before you showed interest. They pulled it up while you were on the phone. They tour homes, collect a commission, and move to the next buyer. They are not looking at the furnace age or the basement walls. They are looking at the calendar.

Buyers who work with agents like this routinely overpay, discover major repair needs after closing, sign buyer agency agreements they cannot exit, and end up at the closing table surprised by numbers they did not see coming. That is what the wrong representation costs.

The Decision

You do not need an agent. You need the right agent. The difference between working with someone who is genuinely in your corner and someone who is managing a transaction on the way to a commission check is tens of thousands of dollars and a level of post-closing stress that no one anticipates when they start their home search.

And if you decide to go without one, go in knowing exactly whose job is whose.

Ask Me

Buyer Representation FAQ

Do buyers have to pay their agent in St. Louis now?

Sometimes. Buyer-agent compensation is negotiable. In many St. Louis sales the seller still pays it, but if the seller doesn’t, your written buyer agreement determines what you owe. Since August 28, 2025, Missouri requires that agreement to be signed before an agent provides brokerage services to you as your agent, so you’ll know the terms up front.

Can I buy a home in St. Louis without a buyer’s agent?

Yes. You can contact the listing agent directly and buy as an unrepresented buyer. The listing agent works for the seller. They must disclose adverse material facts they know or should know about, and if they’re a REALTOR®, treat you honestly, but they won’t advise you on price, terms or strategy.

Will I save money if I go directly to the listing agent?

Not automatically. What the seller pays the listing brokerage is set by the listing agreement, and leaving a buyer’s agent out of the deal doesn’t automatically lower your price. Any savings has to be negotiated.

What is dual agency, and is it allowed in Missouri?

Dual agency is when one agent represents both the buyer and the seller. Missouri allows it only with the consent of all parties. The agent then can’t tell either side the other’s negotiating position, such as that the buyer would pay more or the seller would take less, without that party’s consent. It’s legitimate, but it’s a narrower kind of help than having your own agent.

Does having a buyer’s agent protect my earnest money?

Not by itself. A buyer’s agent explains your options and advocates for you, but the contract obligations, including deadlines and the contingencies you keep or waive, are yours. Buyers with agents can still lose earnest money.
