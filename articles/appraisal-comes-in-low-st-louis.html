What Happens If the Appraisal Comes in Low in St. Louis?

Last updated October 21, 2026

A low appraisal doesn’t end a deal, but it does force a decision about who covers the difference. Here’s what it costs in cash, where it happens in St. Louis, what the contract lets each side do, and what happened when one of my listings appraised low.

George Kindler· Licensed Missouri Realtor· Buyers & Sellers

Low Appraisals in St. Louis, Answered

What happens if the appraisal comes in low in St. Louis?

The lender lends on the lower of the price and the appraised value, so the difference has to come from somewhere: a price reduction, the buyer’s cash, a split, or the end of the contract. Which of those is on the table depends on whether the buyer’s contract has appraisal protection. The buyer’s options ↓

Who pays the difference when a house appraises low?

Nobody automatically. If the buyer has an appraisal contingency, the gap gets negotiated. If they don’t, the buyer covers it or risks the earnest money by walking away. On a $300,000 price that appraises at $285,000, a buyer putting 3% down needs about $14,550 more cash. The math ↓

Can I challenge a low appraisal?

Yes, through the lender, with a request called a reconsideration of value. Bring verified prices on pending sales and the homes actually competing today. It succeeds occasionally. In my experience there’s little recourse even when an appraiser gets it wrong, so build your case before you count on it. How to challenge it ↓

Should the seller lower the price to the appraisal?

Not automatically. First check whether the buyer even has the right to ask: on a St. Louis contract, appraisal protection comes from an appraisal rider, and without one a low appraisal doesn’t open the price back up. Then read the appraisal for errors. The seller’s options ↓

Can I walk away if the appraisal is low?

Only if your contract gives you the right: an appraisal rider, or the amendatory clause that comes with an FHA loan or the escape clause with a VA loan. Without one, walking away puts your earnest money at risk. Should you waive appraisal protection? →

How much cash does a low appraisal cost a buyer?

More than most buyers expect, because the down payment is figured on the appraised value and the gap comes on top of it. Here’s a $300,000 price that appraises at $285,000:

Where do low appraisals happen in St. Louis?

The risk is highest on houses that sell fast and over list, because the appraiser has to find closed sales that support a price the market just set. In 2,865 St. Louis County sales in 2026, the homes that went under contract in their first four days sold for a median 104.0% of the original list price, and 78.7% of them sold above it.

What can a buyer do when the appraisal comes in low?

If your contract has appraisal protection, you have four moves. Without it, you have one: cover the difference and close.

  • Ask the seller to come down to the appraised value, or part of the way.
  • Cover the gap in cash, on top of your down payment, closing costs and reserves.
  • Challenge the appraisal through your lender with better comparable sales.
  • Terminate under the appraisal contingency before its deadline and keep your earnest money.

The clock is short. Once a low appraisal is delivered under an appraisal rider, the seller has 5 days to negotiate, and the buyer then has 2 days to accept or walk away.

The appraisal also has to come back before your loan deadline, or that protection can run out on its own. I push lenders to order it within three days of acceptance. Your deadlines after an accepted offer →

What can a seller do when the appraisal comes in low?

Start before the appraisal. On my listings, when the value matters, I give the appraiser the comparable sales that support the price. After a low appraisal, start with the contract, not the appraisal. On a St. Louis contract, a buyer’s right to renegotiate over value comes from an appraisal rider. If there isn’t one, a low appraisal is the buyer’s problem to solve, and an amendment asking you to lower the price has nothing behind it.

  • No appraisal rider: the price stands. The buyer covers the difference or decides whether to walk away from the earnest money.
  • Appraisal rider in place: it’s a negotiation. Read the appraisal for errors before you agree to anything, and challenge it through the buyer’s lender if the comps support you.
  • FHA or VA buyer: the buyer can walk without losing the earnest money if the value is below the price, and the appraiser’s condition items can become repairs the loan requires.

A price cut also reopens what was already settled. To bring the price down to the appraisal, everything negotiated before it can come back to the table, such as inspection credits and repairs. That has been my approach on low appraisals: the price, the credits and the repairs get negotiated together, not one at a time.

The seller usually sees the appraisal only because the buyer’s side sends it. When it arrives with a request attached, the first question is whether the contract gives them the right to ask.

The Galena appraisal

On 5021 Galena, a listing I sold in 2025, the winning offer was one of four: conventional financing with 20% down and the inspection waived. It was the best offer on paper, netting my sellers more and reducing their risk. It came without a pre-approval letter, and I told my sellers that was the risk when we reviewed it. It also came without an appraisal rider.

The appraisal came in low, and the buyer’s agent sent it to me with an amendment. I asked why: the contract didn’t give her buyer that option. Before going any deeper, I asked whether her client planned to terminate and forfeit the earnest money. In a negotiation, setting the anchor is leverage. My sellers were longtime friends on vacation, and I wanted a solution before I called them with an update.

The same day, the agent agreed they couldn’t ask for a reduction. But the loan had been run as FHA, not the conventional loan in the contract, and the appraisal came with repair conditions. The buyers had the cash, their son did the repairs, and it closed at the contract price of $271,000.

What should a challenge to a low appraisal include?

Through the lender, with facts the appraiser missed. A new appraisal is ordered by the lender, not the agent, and the appraiser rarely moves without new evidence. On Galena I found three problems and challenged every one in writing:

  • Missing features: no credit for the additional parking.
  • Unverified pending sales: the appraiser used pending sales without calling the agents to confirm the price. One was closing $30,000 higher than the appraisal stated.
  • A stale sale: a sale from a year earlier. The principle of substitution asks what a buyer could buy instead today, and there was nothing like this house on the market.

He didn’t change the value, or the condition items: a trip hazard outside any walking path and an unattached downspout, minor items I considered an overreach. In my experience, even when an appraiser doesn’t follow USPAP, the appraisal standards, there’s virtually no recourse. Challenge it when the comps support you, but plan as if the number will stand.

How is a low FHA appraisal different?

An FHA appraiser checks condition as well as value, so a low FHA appraisal can bring repairs along with a lower number. FHA loans also require an amendatory clause that lets the buyer out without losing the earnest money if the value comes in below the price. That’s why the financing type written in the contract matters: Galena’s contract said conventional, and the FHA conditions surfaced only after the appraisal. FHA vs. conventional →

More Questions About Appraisals

Value

Does a low appraisal mean I overpaid?

Not necessarily. An appraisal is built mostly from closed sales, so in a fast market it can trail what buyers are paying now. It does mean the lender won’t finance the difference, and that you’d need the market to keep rising to sell for the same price soon.

What if the house appraises higher than the price?

The price doesn’t change. The lender still lends on the lower of the price and the appraised value, so the buyer starts with more equity on paper, and the seller can’t ask for more.

The seller’s side

Does the seller get a copy of the buyer’s appraisal?

Not automatically. The appraisal is ordered by the buyer’s lender, and the seller usually sees it only when the buyer’s side shares it, often with a request to renegotiate. What a seller can do ↑
