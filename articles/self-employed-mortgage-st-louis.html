Buying a House When You’re Self-Employed: How Lenders Count Your Income

Last updated October 28, 2026

You can get a regular mortgage when you work for yourself. The catch is that the lender doesn’t see what your business brings in; it sees what your tax returns show after write-offs. Enter your last two years below and see the income a lender will count, the house it supports and what your deductions are costing you at the closing table.

Self-Employed Mortgages in St. Louis, Answered

Can you get a mortgage if you’re self-employed?

Yes, the same conventional, FHA and VA loans everyone else gets. The difference is proof: lenders generally want two years of self-employment and two years of tax returns. With less than two years, you can still qualify if your latest return shows a full 12 months from the business and you earned similar income in the same line of work before. How do lenders calculate self-employed income?

From your tax returns, not your deposits. They start with net profit, add back paper expenses like depreciation, and average the last two years. A business netting $115,000 and then $130,000, with $8,000 and $10,000 added back, counts as $131,500 a year, or $10,958 a month. The worked example ↓

Do tax write-offs hurt my mortgage approval?

Yes. Every dollar you deduct is a dollar of income the lender can’t count, unless it’s an add-back like depreciation. In the example on this page, $10,000 more in deductions last year cuts about $25,400 from the house price that fits. What a write-off costs you ↓

What if my income went down last year?

The lender uses the lower year, not the average. If that example business came in at $105,000 last year instead of $140,000, counting add-backs, the price that fits drops from about $587,000 to about $452,000. Should I get a bank statement loan instead?

Only when your write-offs hide most of your income. A bank statement loan counts 12 to 24 months of deposits, usually less a 50% expense factor, but lenders’ published terms call for at least 10% down and a rate typically 0.5 to 1.5 points higher. If your returns already show your income, a regular loan wins. Tax returns vs. bank statements ↓

How does a lender turn my tax return into income?

They run a cash flow analysis. Fannie Mae requires lenders to put it in writing, on its Form 1084 or a worksheet that follows the same rules. It works in three steps.

  1. Start with net profit. What’s left after every expense you deducted.
  2. Add back the paper expenses. Depreciation, depletion, amortization, business use of your home and casualty losses come back in, because they lowered your taxes without taking cash out of your pocket. One-time income, like selling equipment, comes out.
  3. Average two years. If income held steady or grew, the lender averages the two years. If it fell, the lender uses the lower year.

Here’s the example the calculator opens with. A business nets $115,000 two years ago and $130,000 last year, with $8,000 and $10,000 in depreciation and home-office deductions. The lender counts $123,000 and $140,000, averages them to $131,500, and qualifies you on $10,958 a month. With a $600 car payment, 10% down at 6.75% and a 45% debt-to-income limit, that supports about a $587,000 house: $3,426 a month in principal and interest and $4,331 with taxes, insurance and mortgage insurance.

What does a write-off cost me when I buy a house?

About $2.54 of house price for every dollar of deduction, in the example above. Deduct $10,000 more last year and the two-year average drops $5,000; at a 45% debt limit that’s about $25,400 less house. If your income is falling, the lender uses only the latest year, and the same $10,000 costs about twice as much.

That’s the trade every self-employed buyer makes: lower taxes now, or more income on paper for the lender. Don’t make it in April by yourself. If you plan to buy in the next year or two, sit down with your CPA and a lender before you file, so you know what the return needs to show.

My take: talk to a lender a year before you think you’re ready, not a month. I send every buyer to a lender before they go under contract, and for business owners that conversation has to come before the tax return, because once it’s filed, that’s the number.

What happens if my income dropped?

The lender qualifies you on the lower year. Using the same business, if last year’s profit plus add-backs came in at $105,000 instead of $140,000, the lender uses $105,000, not the $114,000 average, and the price that fits drops to about $452,000.

A drop also changes how the file is reviewed. Expect a letter explaining why: a lost client, a slow season, a year you reinvested. When the drop is large, about 20% or more, some lenders won’t approve it until another year of returns shows the income stabilized. If you know last year was down, find that out before you start looking at houses.

How do partners and S-corporation owners qualify?

On the K-1, plus proof the money actually reaches you. Fannie Mae treats anyone who owns 25% or more of a business as self-employed. Partners and S-corporation owners get a K-1 instead of a Schedule C, and the lender counts that income only if your distributions match it, or if the business’s own balance sheet shows it can afford to pay it out.

  • Guaranteed payments to a partner count when you have a two-year history of them.
  • W-2 wages from your own S corporation count like any paycheck, and the K-1 income is added on top.
  • Profit left in the business to grow it may not count at all without the business returns to show it’s there.

Partners in firms where income swings from year to year, or arrives as distributions after the year closes, should bring both years of business returns to the first lender meeting, not just the K-1.

Can I buy a house with less than two years self-employed?

Yes, if you did the same work before. Fannie Mae allows it when your most recent tax return shows a full 12 months of income from the business, and you earned similar or more income in the same field or a similar one before going out on your own. A nurse who opens a practice qualifies sooner than a nurse who opens a restaurant.

FHA works much the same way: two years is the standard, and one to two years can work with prior experience in the same line of work. With less than a year on your own return, most buyers wait.

Is a bank statement loan better than a regular mortgage?

Only when your write-offs hide your real income. A bank statement loan is a non-qualified mortgage: the lender reads 12 or 24 months of deposits, usually subtracts a 50% expense factor for a business account, and qualifies you on the rest. Lenders’ published terms call for at least 10% down, often 15% to 20%, and a rate typically 0.5 to 1.5 points above a conventional loan.

In the calculator’s example, $22,000 a month in deposits less 50% counts as $11,000, almost exactly what the tax returns show. With the higher rate, the bank statement loan supports about $544,000, less than the $587,000 the regular loan does. It only pulls ahead when your deductions are large compared with your deposits. Run both before you pick.

How much cash do I need when I’m self-employed?

The down payment, about 2.5% for closing costs, and six months of house payments left over. In the example, that’s about $73,400 to close and $26,000 in reserve, roughly $99,400 in all.

My take: keep six months of house payments after closing, regardless of what the loan program requires. When your income comes from clients instead of a paycheck, that reserve is what carries you through a slow quarter without touching the mortgage.

Documents and money

What documents does a self-employed buyer need?

Two years of signed personal tax returns with every schedule, and two years of business returns if you own 25% or more of a partnership or corporation. Many lenders also ask for a year-to-date profit and loss statement and recent business bank statements. When the business has been open at least five years, some files need only one year of returns. Is 1099 income self-employment income?

Yes. 1099 pay is reported on Schedule C, so lenders treat it like a business: net profit after expenses, averaged over two years. A contractor whose 1099s add up to $150,000 but who deducts $40,000 in expenses qualifies on about $110,000, plus any add-backs. How the math works ↑

Can I use money from my business for the down payment?

Usually, yes, if you own the business and the lender confirms that taking the cash out won’t hurt it. Expect the lender to review the business’s cash flow before it counts those funds.
