How to Sell a House in St. Louis, Step by Step

Last updated November 10, 2026

Selling a house in St. Louis comes down to ten steps, and most of the money is won or lost before the sign goes up. Here’s the order I take my sellers through, with the 2026 numbers behind each step and a link to the guide that covers it in full.

Selling a House in St. Louis, Answered

What are the steps to sell a house in St. Louis?

Ten, in this order: pick your timing, know your net, walk the house and decide on repairs, check your city’s occupancy rules, price it from the sales that won, get the listing and disclosure ready, launch on a Thursday, work the offers, get through inspection and appraisal, then close. Most of the money is won or lost before the sign goes up. The ten steps ↓

How long does it take to sell a house in St. Louis?

About six to eight weeks from list day to closing when the price is right. The median 2026 St. Louis County sale went under contract in 10 days, and a listing usually closes 30 to 45 days after that. Add time before you list for prep and, where your city requires one, an occupancy inspection that can take a few weeks. How the timeline runs ↓

What should I do first when I decide to sell?

Get your payoff and call your city. Ask your lender for a payoff statement so you know what you’ll walk away with, and if your city requires an occupancy inspection, schedule it as soon as you decide to sell. Then walk the house with your agent before you spend a dollar on repairs. Before you list ↓

What decides whether my house sells fast?

The price against the houses buyers are choosing between. In 2026, St. Louis County houses that sold within 14 days were listed at a median 97.1% of comparable houses’ price per square foot; houses that sat 60 days or more were listed at 104.0% and sold at 94.6%. How to price it →

How much does it cost to sell a house in St. Louis?

On four St. Louis sales I closed in 2026, sellers paid 5.1% to 7.6% of the sale price. I tell sellers to plan on 6% to 10% before the mortgage payoff: commission, title, prorated taxes, repairs or a credit, and prep. The full cost breakdown →

How the timeline usually runs

From the day you decide to sell to the day the money lands, a well-priced St. Louis house usually moves through four stretches:

  • Before you list: as long as your prep, photos and any occupancy inspection take. Maryland Heights can take a few weeks, and St. Louis REALTORS warned in 2024 that County inspection waits could reach four to six weeks.
  • List day to contract: a median 10 days for 2026 St. Louis County sales, and a median 5 days for the 2,235 sellers who never cut their price.
  • Contract to closing: usually 30 to 45 days. The buyer’s inspection period comes first (10 days on my Country Glen listing), then the appraisal and the buyer’s final loan approval.
  • Closing day: your proceeds usually reach your account the same day.

The stretch you control most is the first one. A price buyers act on shortens the second, and a clean contract shortens the third.

Step 1: Pick your timing, and whether you sell or buy first

March or April puts your house in front of the most buyers. Across 2023–2025, St. Louis County listings averaged 10.5 to 10.8 showings in those months, about twice December’s 5.1. If your house has something you can’t change, like a slab foundation, a busy road or a highway behind it, that’s even more reason to list into spring demand. Selling by month →

If you’re also buying, the order matters more than the month. Selling first, I usually suggest listing at the end of winter, around February. Buying first, buy in spring and sell in summer, and have an experienced lender confirm you can carry both payments. Sell first or buy first? →

Step 2: Know what you’ll walk away with

Ask your lender for a payoff statement dated around your expected closing date, and include any home equity line or second mortgage; both get paid off at closing. Then plan on netting about 90 to 94 percent of the sale price before the payoff. On four St. Louis sales I closed in 2026, sellers kept 92.4% to 94.9%.

That number keeps every later decision tied to what actually reaches your bank account: which repairs are worth it, where to price, and which offer to take. Run your net →

Step 3: Walk the house and decide on repairs

Our first meeting starts with the house, not a listing presentation. You tell me the good, the bad and the ugly, and I look for anything that could change how buyers see the value. What I look at →

For every repair I ask three questions. Does it increase or preserve value? Does it remove a roadblock for buyers? Is there a municipal occupancy requirement? If all three answers are no, the money usually stays in your pocket. Don’t replace things just because they’re old: a working older water heater or HVAC system, or a roof replaced on age alone, is the kind of spending I question first. My three questions →

Step 4: Check your city’s occupancy rules

Many St. Louis County cities inspect a house when it changes hands. Unincorporated St. Louis County, Sunset Hills, Green Park, Webster Groves and Maryland Heights require one; Crestwood, Chesterfield and the City of Fenton don’t for an existing owner-occupied home. In Jefferson County, fire and sewer districts drive many resale inspections, and incorporated cities add their own rules.

Your mailing address doesn’t settle it: a Fenton or Webster Groves mailing address doesn’t mean the house is inside that city. Maryland Heights required its inspection on both of my 2026 sales there, and the city can run behind, so I schedule it as soon as a seller decides to sell. Where an inspection is required, the city’s list, not guesswork, tells you what has to be fixed. Occupancy rules by city →

Step 5: Price it from the sales that won

Price against the houses buyers are choosing between right now, not the highest sale on the street or an online estimate. In 2026, St. Louis County houses that sold within 14 days were listed at a median 97.1% of comparable houses’ price per square foot and sold at 101.0%. Houses that sat 60 days or more were listed at 104.0% and sold at 94.6%. On a $300,000 house, that’s about $19,000 less than pricing it right, plus about three months of carrying costs. How to price a house to sell →

Be careful with the agent who quotes the highest number. Ask what evidence is behind it and what happens if buyers don’t respond. And if your Zestimate is well below where we price, I pull it before we list; if it’s higher, I leave it up. Turning off the Zestimate →

Step 6: Get the listing and your disclosure ready

Presentation is the cheapest value you can add. Clean, declutter, and paint only if the walls are dull, faded or scuffed. Then get great photos: buyers decide online whether a house is worth a showing. Improvements that aren’t repairs →

Fill out the seller’s disclosure, and disclose what you know: past water, roof or foundation problems, repairs and insurance claims. Selling as-is doesn’t remove that obligation, and hiding a problem can create liability long after closing. Homes built before 1978 also need the federal lead-based paint disclosure.

Decide what stays. Anything attached to the house goes with it unless the contract excludes it, so if you want to keep a doorbell camera or a light fixture, say so up front instead of removing it before closing.

Step 7: Launch on a Thursday and read the first weekend

I launch listings on a Thursday, with great pictures and a strong price, so buyers can put the house on their weekend lists. Sunday is traditionally a poor day to list, because most buyers have already set their showing schedule. Why Thursday →

The first weekend is your answer. In my experience, multiple offers come that weekend and rarely after. On my 5021 Galena listing in 2025, listed at $250,000, we had 27 showings and four offers in two days. If buyers chose other houses instead, diagnose why right away rather than waiting for the calendar. When to lower the price →

Step 8: Work the offers

Don’t accept the first offer the first evening. I call and text the agents from earlier and upcoming showings and ask the buyer’s agent for 24 to 48 hours, because the first offer usually comes from the most motivated buyer, not the only one. With your consent, I take each offer to the next buyer; I rarely call for highest and best. How I run multiple offers →

Then compare offers by what they net you and how likely they are to close: price minus any seller-paid costs, the buyer’s loan and lender, the inspection and appraisal terms, any home-sale contingency, the earnest money (about 1% is typical with a financed buyer) and the closing date. My job is to make sure you understand every term. You make the decision. How to compare offers →

Step 9: Get through inspection and appraisal

Expect a repair request after the buyer’s inspection. That’s where concessions belong, not on the listing, and in my experience they typically run $2,000 to $8,000. For any repair you agree to make, have written proof ready before the final walk-through, not at the closing table. Budgeting for inspection repairs →

The appraisal comes next. If the buyer’s contract has an appraisal rider and the value comes in low, the current St. Louis contract gives the seller 5 days to negotiate and the buyer 2 days to accept or walk. Without a rider, a low appraisal doesn’t reopen the price: at Galena the appraisal came in at $265,000, and the house still closed at the $271,000 contract price, with the buyer covering the difference. If the appraisal comes in low →

Step 10: Close, hand over the keys and get paid

The St. Louis contract transfers possession at closing unless a rider says otherwise, so plan to be moved out by then. If you need more time, ask for a rent-back in writing, and know the buyer may want something for it in price, concessions or rent. Rent-backs →

At closing, commission, title and settlement fees, prorated property taxes and your payoff come out of the proceeds. Missouri property taxes are paid in arrears, so you credit the buyer for your share of the current year. The money usually reaches your account the same day; an afternoon closing sometimes lands the next morning. What comes out at closing →

More Questions About Selling a House

If it doesn’t go to plan

What if my house doesn’t sell?

Find where buyers stopped before you change anything. In 2026, 630 of 2,865 St. Louis County sellers cut their price, and they sold for a median 91.6% of their original list. A new MLS number doesn’t bring a new pool of buyers; a better reason to act does. On my Covington Gardens relisting, a house that expired at $180,000 sold for $200,000 the next spring, with new photos and no new work. Why a house didn’t sell →

Other ways to sell

Can I sell my house without an agent in Missouri?

Yes. You can sell it yourself (FSBO), and then the pricing, disclosure, MLS exposure, showings, contract, inspection and closing are all yours to run. Many owners get on the MLS through a flat-fee service. Missouri FSBO guide →

Should I sell to a cash buyer instead?

Compare it on net first. In my experience, wholesale cash offers land around 50% of market value, rarely more than 60 to 65 cents on the dollar, so the speed has to be worth what it costs you. What you give up with a cash offer →

Taxes and fees

Is there a transfer tax when I sell a house in Missouri?

No. Missouri doesn’t charge one statewide. You’ll pay deed recording fees, and some cities have their own occupancy or point-of-sale inspection requirements. Title and closing costs →
