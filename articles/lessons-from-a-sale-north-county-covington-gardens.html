What changes when an expired listing sells the second time?

Usually not the house. A relaunch works when it changes what buyers saw and when they saw it: the season, the photos and a price set by fresh comps, not by the last price cut. This house expired at $180,000 and, with no new work, sold for $200,000 the next spring.

Why would a house that sat in late fall sell in spring?

Because the buyers are different in number, not in kind. Demand and prices peak in spring, and a house that needs every buyer in its price range should be on the market when the most buyers are looking. This one went up the last week of October and expired around Thanksgiving; the relaunch hit in mid-May.

How it went on this sale: The relaunch went live as Coming Soon on a Thursday in mid-May, with showings set for another Thursday, June 6. It closed June 28, at the peak of the season. Five years of St. Louis seasonal data →

How do you price a relaunch after a price cut didn’t work?

Start over with comps, and treat the old price cut as one data point, not the floor. Look at like-kind homes two ways: where they were listed to draw buyers in, and where they actually sold. Here the comps put the relaunch at $189,900, $9,900 above the price it expired at.

How it went on this sale: I ran the comps the same way I did on Galena: like-kind houses, where they listed to attract demand and where they closed. The relaunch price was $100 under the first listing’s original $190,000, and the house sold $10,100 over it. Pricing a split-level on stale comps →

What do new photos do when nothing about the house changes?

They decide whether buyers book a showing. Online, the photos are the first showing; buyers who don’t like them never see the house. This relaunch had no new work, only a new set of photos of the same rooms, the same deck and the same yard.

How it went on this sale: The seller told me the first listing’s photos were taken on a phone. I went back and reshot the house myself: 25 photos, including the deck and the finished lower level the first listing barely showed. Why the first weekend matters →

How does a house go under contract before it leaves Coming Soon?

By giving buyers a launch date to plan around. Coming Soon puts the listing in front of buyers and agents before showings start, so the ones who want it are ready on day one. This house spent three weeks in Coming Soon and went under contract the day showings were set to begin, with 0 days on market.

How it went on this sale: Coming Soon on Thursday, May 16; showings set for Thursday, June 6; contract signed June 6. Because the house had been off the market more than 30 days, its cumulative days on market started over at zero, so buyers saw a fresh listing, not one that had sat. How do you handle inspection repairs when the house also needs an occupancy permit?

Price the repairs before you answer. In unincorporated St. Louis County, the county requires an occupancy permit when a new owner moves in, so the seller can face two repair lists at once: the county’s and the buyer’s. Get bids on the buyer’s requests first, so the seller decides on real numbers inside the inspection deadline. Here the repair and occupancy work came to $4,250, paid by the seller at closing.

How it went on this sale: The buyer used an FHA loan. On June 17 I recommended we get bids on the requested repairs before responding. On June 21 the seller agreed to complete them. The work was paid out of the seller’s proceeds at closing, and the seller gave no concessions. How repair requests affect the price →

What happens when the inspection agreement isn’t clearly delivered?

It can stall a closing. An inspection agreement counts only when it’s signed by both sides and delivered, so confirm delivery in writing the day it’s signed and keep the record. On this sale, two days before closing, the two sides disagreed about whether the properly signed agreement had been delivered.

How it went on this sale: The question came up June 25 and 26. It was sorted out, and the sale closed on schedule on Friday, June 28, 2024, at $200,000. What happens between contract and keys →

What should a seller with an expired listing take from this sale?

Before you change the house, change what buyers see and when they see it. This one sold for $20,000 more than it expired at with no new work.

Did this house need another price cut?

No. The comps put it $9,900 above where it expired, and buyers paid $10,100 more than that.

Was it worth waiting until spring?

For this seller, yes. The first listing ran from late October to late November. The relaunch came six months later, in mid-May, and went under contract on the first day of showings.

Do the photos matter more than the house?

Not more, but first. Buyers decide whether to see a house from the photos. The same house, reshot, drew a buyer before it ever went active.
