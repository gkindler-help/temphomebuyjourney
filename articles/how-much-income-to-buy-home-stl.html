How Much Income Do You Need to Buy a Home in St. Louis?

Last updated October 13, 2026

Your lender's approval number and your real affordability number are not the same thing. What the math actually looks like across St. Louis price points -- and what lenders will not tell you.

How much income do I need to buy a home in St. Louis?

To keep the whole payment at a comfortable 28% of gross income, roughly $55,000 a year for a $150,000 home, $92,000 for $250,000 and $110,000 for $300,000, at a 7% rate with 5% down. Lenders may approve you on much less, which isn’t the same as being able to afford it. See the full table ↓

How much income do I need for a $200,000 home in St. Louis?

About $73,400 a year to keep the payment at 28% of gross income, at a 7% rate with 5% down. A lender could approve it on about $47,800 if you have no other debts, but that’s a ceiling, not a comfortable budget.

Pre-Approved vs. Actually Affordable

Because a pre-approval measures the most you can borrow, not the payment you can live with. Lenders calculate your maximum loan amount based on your debt-to-income ratio (DTI) -- total monthly debts generally can’t exceed roughly 43% to 50% of your gross monthly income, depending on the loan. They use gross income, not take-home pay, and they may estimate property taxes before you’ve picked the house.

The result is a pre-approval number that represents the maximum you can borrow -- not the payment you can sustain comfortably over 30 years in a specific St. Louis neighborhood.

St. Louis Tax Variation Matters Property taxes in the St. Louis area vary by city, school district and whether you’re in the City or the County, so the same price can carry a different payment a few miles away. Check the specific address before you settle on a budget, using affordability by ZIP code and 2026 property taxes by school district.

Debt-to-Income in Practice

For most loans, total monthly debts, including the new housing payment, of about 43% of gross income or less, and up to 50% on many conventional loans approved through automated underwriting. FHA can go higher with compensating factors. Those are maximums, not targets: the 28% housing guideline in the table is the conservative number, and lenders will often approve buyers below it.

The question is not what the lender will approve. The question is what you can actually sustain when a $6,000 HVAC replacement comes up in year 3, or when a job disruption creates a few months of financial pressure. Being approved for the maximum and buying the maximum are two very different decisions.

What Lenders Leave Out

The costs of owning that aren’t debts: maintenance, utilities and the real tax bill on the specific house.

  • The actual tax bill: Taxes differ by city and school district, so two homes at the same price can carry different payments. Check the address, not an average.
  • Maintenance reserves: A 1% of value annual maintenance reserve on a $250,000 home is $208/month. This is not in any lender's DTI calculation. It is a real cost of homeownership.
  • HOA fees: Some St. Louis properties carry HOA fees of $100 to $500+ per month. Lenders include this in DTI calculations if disclosed, but buyers often discover HOAs late in the process.
  • Utilities change: Moving from an apartment to a house means heating and cooling a larger space. On older St. Louis housing stock with original insulation, utility costs can exceed what buyers expect.

Two Incomes, Two Debt Loads

Yes, but both debt loads count too. If you are buying with a partner, both incomes count toward qualifying. A household where Partner A earns $55,000 and Partner B earns $45,000 has $100,000 combined gross income and qualifies at a significantly higher price point than either individually. However, if Partner B has $600/month in student loan payments, that reduces the available DTI cushion for housing costs.

Run the math as a household, not individually, and include all recurring debts: car payments, student loans, minimum credit card payments, personal loans.

Finding Your Real Number

Stress-test it. The right question is not “what is the maximum I can be approved for?” It is "what monthly payment can I sustain for 30 years across different life scenarios?" Run the numbers with a 10% income reduction, a major unexpected repair, and a new car payment. If the housing payment is still manageable in those scenarios, you have found your real number -- not your maximum approval.

How lenders count income and debt

Do lenders use gross or take-home pay?

Gross. Lenders qualify you on income before taxes and deductions, which is one reason an approval can feel bigger than your budget. Build your own budget on take-home pay.

How do student loans count in my debt-to-income?

Your documented monthly payment counts. If the payment shows as $0 or the loan is deferred, FHA counts 0.5% of the balance each month instead, so a $40,000 balance counts as $200. Conventional rules vary by agency, so get a statement from your servicer showing your actual payment before you apply.

How much should I budget for maintenance?

About 1% of the home’s value a year, or roughly $208 a month on a $250,000 house. Lenders don’t count it, and older St. Louis houses often need more.
