How I Work With St. Louis Home Buyers

Talk first. Then a lender. Text me the address. Walk the house for what costs money. Two numbers before every offer. Then you decide.

Buying With George, Answered

What’s the first step to buying a house with George?

A conversation. We get on the phone or meet in person, usually for about an hour, and you tell me your goals, what you want in a house and where you’d like to buy. I walk you through the timeline, then email you a summary with your first prep step: talking to a lender.

The first conversation ↓

Do I have to sign a buyer agreement before George shows me a house?

Yes, before I start working for you as your agent, but not before you’re confident we’re a good fit. I always offer to walk an open house with you first. That’s an interview, not representation, so it doesn’t take a signed agreement. When we do sign, it’s at a rate that makes sense, and Missouri has required that written agreement since August 28, 2025.

The agreement ↓

How does George keep me from wasting time and money on the wrong house?

Text me the address before you schedule, and I’ll tell you what I see before you give up a Saturday. At the showing, I help you spot the deal breakers before you pay for an inspection.

Before the showing ↓

Will George tell me what to offer?

I’ll give you two numbers: what I think the appraiser will value the home at, and where the market will likely land, within a $10,000 range. Comps come first, concessions second. What you write is your call.

The offer ↓

Will George push me to offer more or move faster?

No. I’m on your timeline, and I hate hard sales tactics. It’s your purchase and you’re the one on the hook for the mortgage, so my job is to get you every piece of information I can find, then let you decide.

How I approach every offer ↓

Answers reflect George’s own process as a licensed Missouri agent. Missouri’s written buyer-agreement requirement took effect August 28, 2025.

If you’re thinking about buying a house, I’m not going to start by sending you a stack of listings and a schedule of Saturday showings.

Finding the house isn’t the hard part anymore. You can do that from your phone. My job shows up before and after you find one you like: what you can really afford, what the listing isn’t telling you, what the house is worth, and what it takes to get it to closing.

Buyer education is my favorite part of this job. Here’s what working with me looks like.

1 FIRST CONVERSATION

What do we cover in our first conversation?

Before you talk to a lender, we get on a phone call or meet in person. You tell me about your goals, what you’d like to find in a house and where you’d like to buy. I walk you through the home buying timeline and what to expect at each step.

That conversation usually runs about an hour. Not because I demand it, but because buyers have a lot of questions, and I love answering them. We typically cover:

How healthy is the market right now?
How do showings work?
What happens at the inspection?
How do we negotiate?
Which homes fit my goals?
What’s the timeline from here to closing?

Don’t sweat remembering all of it. After we talk, I send you an email that covers our conversation and your prep steps, starting with talking to a lender.

I’m on your timeline. I’m not rushing you.

I hate hard sales tactics. I look at myself as a counseling advocate. Ultimately this is your purchase, and you’re the one on the hook for the mortgage, not me.

2 LENDER

Why talk to a lender before I look at houses?

Because the lender tells us what you qualify for, and together we keep anything on your credit from changing that before closing.

After our first conversation, I send every buyer to a lender. The lender and I go over what not to touch on your credit and how any change would affect what you can buy. That’s why credit surprises rarely happen to my clients. Hold big purchases like a car or furniture until after closing.

Get pre-approved before we tour, keep the letter current and choose a lender whose process I trust to close on time. A deal that falls apart in underwriting costs everyone.

Then we talk about what the number means. The pre-approval amount is the most the lender will loan you under their guidelines. It isn’t necessarily what you should spend. I want you to keep six months of house payments in reserve after your down payment and closing costs, whatever the loan program requires.

3 THE PLAN

How do we decide when to buy and how hard to compete?

By your budget, how strong a borrower you are and how much you want to compete. “Is now a good time to buy?” doesn’t have one answer. It depends on you, so I’m strategic about it buyer by buyer.

Stretching your budget in a competitive price range

Shop in the fall, or look at homes that have been sitting.

Strong borrower in a range with options

Fall or spring can both work.

Want to compete less?

Narrow the must-haves. That does more than waiting for the perfect month.

If you also have a house to sell, the order matters. Selling first, I’d list at the end of winter. Buying first, buy in the spring and sell in the summer.

Can we walk a house together before I sign anything?

Yes. I always offer to walk an open house with you first. I’m not working as your agent there. It’s an interview, and it’s educational, the same as my YouTube videos or this site, and none of those need an agency agreement. You get to see how I look at a house before you commit to anything.

We sign a buyer agency agreement, absolutely. Missouri requires one before I start working for you as your agent. But not before you’re confident we’ll work well together, and it’s for a rate that makes sense.

4 THE ADDRESS

What do you check before I schedule a showing?

Text me the address first. Before we schedule, I verify with the listing agent whether the seller is offering buyer-agent compensation, and how much. I pull the price history, days on market, the seller’s disclosure, the listing photos and the showing calendar. Five minutes with the county permit record tells me whether work was done legally. If you’re using an FHA or VA loan, many times I can tell there are going to be conforming issues before we ever set foot inside.

Then I tell you yes, it’s worth scheduling, or here’s what I’m seeing that concerns me. I tell you what I see before you give up a Saturday.

5 THE SHOWING

What are you looking for when we walk a house?

What costs money, not paint. What I’m looking for changes the moment I know what kind of house we’re walking. You’re deciding whether you want to live there. I’m looking at the mechanicals, the basement, the electrical panel and the exterior.

I decode the furnace and air conditioner serial numbers at the showing, so I know their age before the inspector does. If I see a Federal Pacific panel, peeling paint on an older house or a major foundation issue, I tell you before we write. I won’t let my buyers write an offer on a home that won’t close.

I tell every buyer: it’s your job to fall in love with the house. It’s my job to make sure you’re not falling in love with a disaster, and that you’re not spending money when you shouldn’t.

That’s why I help you identify deal breakers before you pay for an inspection. When you do spend the money, the inspector isn’t pointing out what was obvious at the showing. They’re looking at what you can’t see: the systems, the roof and under the basement floor.

And the things I find aren’t just warnings. Found before the offer, they’re negotiating tools. It changes the conversation: I’m not reacting to the inspection report, I’m prepared for it.

6 BEFORE WE WRITE

What do you ask the listing agent before we write an offer?

How much activity they’ve had, whether there are offers in hand, whether the seller is flexible on timing or terms, what issues I should know about and whether the seller is open to repairs if the offer makes sense. The moment you give me permission, I’m on the phone. Most listing agents tell you more than you expect if you ask directly.

How much activity have you had?
Any offers in hand or expected?
Is the seller flexible on timeline or terms?
Any property issues I should know before we write?
If the offer makes sense, is the seller open to repairs?

I also want to know we’re working with a reasonable seller before we present the offer. So I make the listing agent aware of the conditions we saw at the showing and ask whether the seller is open to repairs if the initial offer makes sense. Their answer tells us a lot about how the inspection negotiation will go.

I put that next to the showing count, the days on market and the comparable sales. Sometimes the answer changes the whole offer. I once wrote an offer that looked below list on paper but won, because I found out the seller needed a 60-day close and we could give them that.

7 THE OFFER

How do you figure out what the house is worth?

I start with the full last year of comparable sales. Then I give you two numbers: what I think the appraiser will value the home at, and what the market will likely land on, within a $10,000 range. Sometimes they’re the same. When they aren’t, the gap is what you need to understand before you write.

You will never feel pressure from me. Not to write a higher purchase price. Not to move faster than you’re comfortable with.

Comps first, concessions second. If you need concessions, I structure the offer price to account for them instead of layering them on top of an already-low number. My position is always to keep more cash in your pocket, and stale listings are a great way to do it. Days on market sets your leverage, not your opening number.

When there really are multiple offers, I’ll use an escalation clause with a hard cap. An appraisal gap guarantee is different: it can win the house, but only after I’ve asked the listing agent the right questions about the competition, and only when you have the cash to cover it. For a financed purchase, earnest money is typically about 1% of the price.

Will I have to pay your commission?

Rarely, but it can happen. I’ll be honest with you. Since the NAR settlement, your buyer agreement states what I’m paid, and you’re responsible for anything the seller doesn’t cover. In St. Louis, that’s nothing new for my buyers: since 2013, they’ve signed agency agreements with a stated amount.

It’s still very rare that a buyer pays my commission. Not because it’s guaranteed, but because the net number is what I focus on when I present the offer, not which column the debits and credits land in.

On a recent sale, my buyers did pay the commission, and their total still came in about $25,000 under their $350,000 budget.

We ran into a rare seller who didn’t want to pay buyer-agent compensation, even though the net number made sense. So it does happen. But I’ve only had buyers pay it when all the numbers worked. Read how that sale played out →

8 UNDER CONTRACT

What happens once we’re under contract?

Once we’re under contract, I set reminders on a calendar we share and track every deadline on my end. On day one, schedule the inspection for day 3 or 4, not day 8, so the results come back with time to make decisions.

I need you to answer, too. If I’m reaching out three or four days before a deadline and can’t get a response, I can’t protect you from a waiver. Keep your credit and your bank accounts exactly as the lender saw them until after closing.

9 INSPECTION

What do we ask the seller to fix after the inspection?

It depends on the item. Anything major, or anything that affects the loan, occupancy or moving in, gets repaired before closing. Small items can be a credit or a home warranty, and working items with nothing to fix get a home warranty.

Before that, get as many inspections as you can afford. Each one has found something that saved my clients thousands. That includes a sewer scope on every St. Louis home, whatever its age or area. A scope runs $150 to $300. A sewer lateral replacement typically runs $4,000 to $12,000 and can exceed $15,000 in some cases.

I go through every report with you before we respond. Safety hazards go to the top of the list. Here’s how I match the remedy to the item:

Repair before closing

Major items, and anything that affects the loan, occupancy or moving in without worry. A credit doesn’t fix the problem; it makes the seller’s repair yours.

Credit or home warranty

Small items you can live with for a while. A credit can make more sense than a handyman.

Home warranty

Working items with nothing to repair, like a dated furnace that still runs.

When I represent a buyer, I want major repairs completed before closing.

10 WALK-THROUGH & CLOSING

What should I watch for at the walk-through and closing?

Proof of the repairs, and the wire. For any repair the seller agreed to make, I ask for written documentation before the final walk-through, not at the closing table. If something new turns up at the walk-through, call me right away.

Then the rule I give every client on every deal: before you wire a single dollar, call your title company at a phone number you looked up yourself. Not a number from an email. Verify the account, verify the amount, then wire.

I watched an experienced investor lose $27,000 two days before closing to wiring instructions that looked exactly like the title company’s.

Nobody caught it, because nobody called to verify. Earnest money or down payment, verify before you wire, every single time.

Whose side are you on when you represent me?

Yours. I protect my client, while also being transparent and fair to the other party. But representation doesn’t look like equal action or advice.

Same house, same inspection report: when I represent the seller, I’m reducing risk and making sure the deal closes, so I usually steer toward a credit. When I represent you, I’m reducing your risk and making sure the agreed repairs are done before closing. Different client, different advice.

Loans and programs

Does George work with FHA and VA buyers?

Yes. FHA and VA hold the property to a higher safety standard than a conventional loan, so I look for conforming issues from the listing and at the showing, before we write. VA buyers usually have more room to get a loan commitment than FHA buyers.
