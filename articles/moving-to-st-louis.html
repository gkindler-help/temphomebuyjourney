Moving to St. Louis? What to Know Before You Buy a House

Last updated October 30, 2026

Moving to St. Louis is easier than buying here from somewhere else. The city and the county are separate governments, the county is split into dozens of municipalities with their own inspection rules, and the same budget buys very different houses a few miles apart. Here’s what to know before you buy, with 2026 numbers, and how one of my buyers bought a house here without ever touring it with me in person.

Why does it matter whether a house is in the City, the County or a municipality?

Because each one sets its own rules for selling and buying a house. St. Louis City split from St. Louis County in 1876 and is its own government. The county has 88 municipalities, each with its own inspection and permit rules, plus unincorporated areas the county itself governs. When a house sells, the occupancy inspection, who does it and what has to be fixed can all change from one side of a street to the other.

  • Unincorporated St. Louis County requires a county occupancy permit when a new occupant moves in after a sale.
  • Municipalities run their own inspections, and the lists differ city by city.
  • Jefferson County towns add fire and sewer district rules, and incorporated towns have their own occupancy requirements.

How it went on a real sale: On a 2025 VA purchase in Northwoods, the city’s occupancy inspection produced its own repair list. I put that list next to my buyer’s inspection report, because the city’s items and the buyer’s concerns weren’t the same. Occupancy list vs. inspection report →

What does a St. Louis house cost in 2026?

Less than most metros, but the range is wide and it moves by ZIP code. St. Louis County’s median sale price peaked at $348,900 on June 2026 closings. In Jefferson County, 2026 community medians ran from $222,000 in De Soto to $469,900 in Eureka.

The same price buys different houses. A move-in-ready house ran about $259,000 in Affton (63123); in Oakville and Mehlville (63129), move-in ready was about $360,000, and $300,000 bought an as-is house. Price alone won’t tell you what you’re getting until you know the ZIP. What $300K, $450K and $600K buy, by ZIP →

How much are property taxes in St. Louis?

On 2026 sales, the median yearly tax bill was $3,624 across 22 St. Louis County-area school districts and $2,084 across 12 Jefferson County districts.

The bill follows the address: the school district, the municipality and the county’s assessed value. Use the listing’s tax figure to compare areas, then confirm the exact address with the St. Louis County Collector of Revenue before you offer. 2026 tax bills by school district →

How do you buy a St. Louis house from out of state?

With a lender lined up first, and an agent who becomes your eyes on the house. The steps that matter:

  1. Get preapproved before you look. A lender works by phone and email, and an offer from out of state needs proof you can close.
  2. Have your agent walk it on live video, and record it in a form you can revisit: 360 photos or a private 3D tour.
  3. Use the inspection to see what video can’t. The inspector’s report and photos fill the gaps.
  4. Do the final walk-through on video, so you see the condition yourself before you sign.
  5. Close remotely. The title company can arrange it, and someone you trust can pick up the keys.

How it went on a real sale: My buyer was living in Denver, moving back home to St. Louis, and saw the house once, at an open house. Then came the text: “Hey, I want to write an offer on this home.” We never toured together. I walked the house on a live video call, brought my 360 camera and saved the photos to a shared folder so the buyer could look through the house anytime. Before closing, I did the final walk-through on video to confirm the condition. The buyer closed remotely, and a family member picked up the keys. That’s the purchase where the seller took $30,000 off before inspections. Going forward I’m using private 3D tours, which organize and host the walk-through better than a folder of photos. When is the best time to move to St. Louis and buy?

It depends on whether you need a house or a deal. Spring has the most houses and the most competition: in 2026 showings per listing peaked in March, homes sold in a median 7 days from April through July, and the median price peaked on June closings. Fall and winter have fewer houses and fewer buyers to compete with.

If your move date is fixed, start with a lender and an agent two to three months ahead, so you’re ready to write when the right house comes up. Five years of St. Louis seasonal data →

Should you rent first when you move to St. Louis?

Rent first if you don’t know where you’ll work or how long you’ll stay. Buying costs money to get in and money to get out, and a short stay rarely covers both. If you know the area and expect to stay several years, buying usually wins here, because St. Louis prices are low compared with rents.

A buyer moving back home already knows the map. Someone new to St. Louis often doesn’t, and a year renting can be the cheapest way to learn it. Renting vs. buying in St. Louis →

What surprises people buying their first St. Louis house?

Older houses, sewer lines and local rules. Much of the housing is decades old: across North County’s 2026 sales, the median house was built in 1956. That means older electrical, plumbing and foundations, and repairs that newer metros rarely see.

  • Sewer laterals. The line from the house to the street is the owner’s. I recommend a sewer scope on every St. Louis home, at $150 to $300; replacing a lateral typically runs $4,000 to $12,000 and can exceed $15,000.
  • Occupancy inspections that change by municipality, covered above.
  • Appraisals aren’t inspections. Even a VA appraisal can miss a condition problem your inspector will find.
