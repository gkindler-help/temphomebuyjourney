Check the government

Is the house in St. Louis, or just near it?

“The listing says St. Louis. Isn’t that one city?”

No. St. Louis City and St. Louis County split in 1876 and are separate governments. The County is split again into 88 cities, towns and villages, plus unincorporated areas the County runs itself. Which one a house sits in decides your earnings tax, your tax bill and the inspection the house needs before a sale.

A mailing address won’t tell you. A Florissant or Fenton address can sit in unincorporated St. Louis County, and “Fenton” alone can mean three different governments. Check the municipality on the County’s map or property record before you offer.

  • St. Louis City: its own city, 1% earnings tax
  • A County municipality: its own occupancy and permit rules
  • Unincorporated County: the County’s rules

Check the area

Which part of St. Louis fits you?

“We have a budget and a job address. Where do we even start?”

Start with what you’re optimizing for: commute, budget, house age or walkability. Each part of the region trades those differently, and the same price buys a different house a few miles apart. In 2026, a move-in-ready house ran about $259,000 in Affton (63123) and about $360,000 in Oakville and Mehlville (63129), where $300,000 bought an as-is house.

Across St. Louis County, the median sale price peaked at $348,900 on June 2026 closings. Jefferson County community medians ran from $222,000 in De Soto to $469,900 in Eureka. Check the rules

What will the city require when you buy?

“We’re under contract. What does the city have to sign off on?”

It depends on the city. Unincorporated St. Louis County, Sunset Hills, Green Park, Webster Groves and Maryland Heights require an occupancy inspection or permit when a house changes hands; Crestwood, Chesterfield and the City of Fenton don’t for an existing owner-occupied home. Usually the seller orders it, and the wait can run weeks.

The city’s list isn’t your home inspection. On a 2025 Northwoods purchase, the city’s occupancy failures and my buyer’s inspection report didn’t cover the same problems, so I put them side by side before we agreed to repairs.

Check the cost

What will it cost to own a house here?

“The price looks low compared with home. What am I missing?”

The tax bill and, in the City, an earnings tax. On 2026 sales, the median yearly property tax bill was $3,624 across 22 St. Louis County-area school districts and $2,084 across 12 Jefferson County districts. St. Louis City adds a 1% earnings tax for anyone who lives there, and for anyone who works there on what they earn in the City.

Missouri also taxes cars as personal property every year, in the City and the County alike. Compare the full bill for the exact address and the job, not “the City” or “the County” in general.

Price + tax bill + earnings tax (City) + car tax = the real number.

Check the house

What’s different about St. Louis houses?

“Where we’re from, the houses were built in the last 20 years.”

Age. In 2,872 St. Louis County sales in 2026, the median house was built in 1963; 43% were built before 1960 and three of every four before 1980. It varies a lot by area: the median was 1930 in the Maplewood-Richmond Heights district, 1950 in Lemay, 1979 around Oakville High, 1982 in Chesterfield and 1993 across Jefferson County. Older houses mean older electrical, plumbing and foundations, and a sewer lateral, the line from the house to the street, that belongs to the owner. I recommend a sewer scope on every St. Louis home, at $150 to $300; replacing a lateral typically runs $4,000 to $12,000 and can exceed $15,000.

Don’t count on the appraisal to catch it. On a 2025 VA purchase, the appraiser missed moisture and mold under a sink that our inspector found, and a sewer line the seller said was replaced the year before was blocked.

Basements. Many St. Louis houses sit on one, a full level under the main floor that buyers from slab-built places such as Texas don’t have. Square footage on a St. Louis listing generally counts the floors above ground, so an unfinished basement is space on top of the number; on any single listing, ask whether a finished basement is included. Then inspect it: water stains, cracks in the foundation walls and the sewer line all show up down there.

Check the process

Can you buy a house here before you move here?

“We can’t fly in every weekend. Can we still buy?”

Yes, with a lender lined up first and an agent who becomes your eyes on the house. Get preapproved before you look, have your agent walk the house on live video and record it so you can revisit it, use the inspection to see what video can’t, do the final walk-through on video, and close remotely through the title company.

Check the timing

When should you move, and should you rent first?

“Do we buy now, or rent for a year and learn the area?”

Rent first if you don’t know where you’ll work or how long you’ll stay; buying costs money to get in and money to get out. If you’re buying, spring has the most houses and the most competition: in 2026, homes sold in a median 7 days from April through July and the median price peaked on June closings. Fall and winter have fewer houses and fewer buyers.

If your move date is fixed, line up a lender and an agent two to three months ahead, so you’re ready to write when the right house comes up.
