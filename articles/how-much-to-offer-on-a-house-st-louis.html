How Much Should I Offer on a House in St. Louis? What 2026 Sales Show

Last updated October 21, 2026

There’s no single percentage to offer below or above asking in St. Louis. Start with the comparable sales. Then let days on market tell you how much leverage you have: in 2026, homes that sold in their first 4 days got a median 104% of their original list price, and homes that sat 91 days or more got 88%.

Quick Answers

Source: 2,865 closed single-family sales in St. Louis County, January 1–October 5, 2026, each measured against its original list price.

How much should I offer on a house in St. Louis?

Start with the comparable sales, not the list price. Then use days on market to judge your leverage. In 2026, the median home that sold within 4 days got 104% of its original list price; by two weeks the median was right at list, and after 90 days it was 88%. See the 2026 table →

How much below asking should I offer?

It depends on how long the house has sat. In the first week, offering below asking rarely works: 78.7% of homes that sold within 4 days went above list. After 60 days, the median sale was 93.4% of original list or less. My approach on a stale listing: comps first, concessions second, and keep more cash in your pocket. Should I offer above asking price?

Sometimes. On a well-priced home in its first few days, over asking is often what it takes: in 2026, 78.7% of homes that sold within 4 days went above list. Paying over asking isn’t overpaying. Overpaying is going past what the comps support and the number you set before you toured. When there are multiple offers →

How much earnest money should I offer?

About 1% of the price is typical for a financed buyer on a retail sale in St. Louis. On a cash offer I’ve always recommended 10%. A larger deposit can strengthen a financed offer in a competitive situation, but it’s money at risk if you walk away outside your contingencies. How earnest money fits your cash to close →

Can I make my offer stronger without waiving the inspection?

Yes. Keep the right to walk away but drop the repair requests, inspect only specific items, or agree not to ask for anything under $250 an item, and get a sewer scope on every St. Louis home. How I Offer on a House That’s Been Sitting

Comps first, concessions second. I start with what similar homes nearby actually sold for, adjusted for condition. That sets the price. Then I look at days on market and price history to decide what else to ask for, usually seller-paid closing costs or a repair credit.

My position is always to keep more cash in your pocket, and a stale listing is one of the best ways to do it. A seller who has watched the house sit for 60 days is thinking about another month of payments, and that’s when a clean offer at a fair price with help on closing costs gets a yes. When the House Has Multiple Offers

St. Louis doesn’t move as one market. The table above shows it: 990 homes sold within 4 days at a median 104% of list, while 390 sat more than 60 days. The well-priced, move-in-ready houses still draw multiple offers in a weekend. The ones that need work, or were priced high, sit.

When you’re in a multiple-offer situation, compete on terms as well as price:

  • Get fully underwritten, not just pre-approved. In a tie, the surer close wins. Pre-approval vs. underwriting →
  • Match the seller’s timeline. A fast close, or a slower one with a rent-back, can be worth real money to them.
  • Earnest money. A larger deposit signals you’re serious.
  • Tighten contingencies instead of removing them. A shorter inspection window keeps you protected.

When a seller calls for highest and best, you don’t have to go up. If your agent knows where you stand, you can stand on your offer. Use an escalation clause with a hard cap

An escalation clause says you’ll beat competing offers by a set amount, up to a maximum you choose. In a true multiple-offer situation, it means you pay just enough to win instead of guessing high. Set the cap from your budget and the comps before you tour, and ask for proof of the competing offer that triggered it. Know the trade-off: an escalation clause shows the seller your ceiling, so I only use one when the situation warrants it.

Offer an appraisal gap guarantee only with cash to cover it

It depends. In a real multiple-offer situation, an appraisal gap guarantee is an excellent way to reduce the seller’s risk, and that can win the house. But I’d hate to increase a buyer’s exposure when the competition may be inflated.

Here’s how that happens, from my listing experience. In strong areas, investors often write offers that aren’t retail offers. The listing agent can honestly say they have an offer in hand, and they don’t have to tell you it’s a junk offer. So before you guarantee a gap, your agent should ask the right questions about what you’re really competing against. And only guarantee a gap you can cover in cash on top of your down payment, closing costs and reserves. Waiving appraisal protection altogether is the move most likely to leave you owing more than the house is worth.

Set your walk-away number in writing

Decide your ceiling from your comfortable payment and the comps, and write it down before you tour. If the bidding passes it, you’re done. That isn’t losing; that’s the plan working. Find your number with the buying power calculator →

On a move-in-ready home you can shorten the inspection window or agree to raise only major findings, and stay competitive without going in blind. Add a sewer scope on every St. Louis home. Houses that have been sitting

Why has this house been sitting on the market?

Usually price, condition or both, and sometimes access or photos. Ask your agent for the price history and showing activity, and read the disclosure. A house that sat because of price is an opportunity; one that sat because of a known defect needs an inspection before you pick a number. How to find and negotiate on homes that have sat →

Should I make an offer on a house with no competing offers?

Yes, if it fits and the comps support your number. No competition is when you have the most room on price and terms. In 2026, homes that sat more than 30 days sold for a median 97.4% of original list or less.

Is a recent price cut a buying opportunity or a warning sign?

Often an opportunity. In 2026, sellers who cut their price took a typical 6% cut and then sold for a median 97.8% of the reduced price. Find out why it needed the cut before you assume the new price is the floor.

After the offer

What if the house appraises below my offer?

Your lender lends on the lower of the price and the appraised value, so the difference has to be solved. With appraisal protection in your contract, you can negotiate, cover it, split it or cancel; without it, you cover it or risk your earnest money. On a VA loan the rules are different.
