How Repair Costs Should Affect Your Offer Price in St. Louis

Last updated October 18, 2026

Most buyers either ignore repair costs at offer time or overreact to them. Here is the framework for turning what you find during a showing into a defensible offer number -- and how to use the inspection to protect it.

The Framework

Repair costs affect your offer in one of two ways: you price them into your initial offer by reducing what you are willing to pay, or you offer at list and negotiate a repair credit or price reduction after the inspection. Which approach is right depends on the specific situation.

Pricing repairs in upfront makes sense when: the defect is visible during a showing, you have a reasonable cost estimate, and the market is balanced or buyer-favorable (home has been sitting 30+ days, similar homes available). It signals that you did your homework and sets realistic expectations from the start.

Negotiating after inspection makes sense when: you need the inspector's professional documentation to substantiate the cost, the market is competitive and an aggressive initial offer is needed to get under contract, or the issues are not visible until the inspector accesses specific systems.

Pricing In vs. Asking for a Credit

When to price repairs into your initial offer

  • Visible roof condition issues (missing shingles, obvious age)
  • Failed tuckpointing evident on exterior walkthrough
  • Known HVAC age (listing disclosure or agent confirmation)
  • Obvious basement water evidence (staining, efflorescence)

When to offer at list and negotiate post-inspection

  • Issues not identifiable until the inspector accesses the system (sewer scope, electrical panel internals, attic condition)
  • Competitive multiple-offer situations where a discounted initial offer loses the deal
  • Situations where you want the inspector's professional cost estimate to support your ask

Credit, Price Reduction or Repair: Item by Item

I go through every report with my clients before we respond, and we sort it into three lists. Most buyers see the full report and want to ask for everything. That’s the wrong move. A targeted request is more likely to succeed and less likely to blow up the deal.

1. Go/no-go items: hold firm or walk

These aren’t negotiating points; they decide whether the deal works. Any one of them can kill a financed deal if it isn’t addressed before closing.

  • Federal Pacific or Zinsco electrical panels
  • Active foundation movement (moving cracks, not old ones) and horizontal cracks in block walls
  • Evidence of active sewer failure
  • A roof at the true end of its life with no remaining coverage
  • Knob-and-tube or aluminum branch wiring on an FHA or VA loan
  • Active water intrusion, structural problems, and anything that would keep you from getting a mortgage or homeowners insurance

Remedy: a seller repair before closing when your lender requires it, otherwise a credit or price reduction big enough to cover it. If the seller won’t, walking away is a business decision, not a failure.

2. Negotiating points: usually a credit

  • An older roof with limited remaining life
  • An HVAC system or water heater at the end of its service range
  • An active termite infestation (old treated damage with a clear current report is informational)
  • Safety items such as missing handrails, exposed wiring, missing GFCI outlets near water, and smoke or CO detectors

For systems near the end of their life, I don’t ask the seller to replace them. I ask for a credit for the remaining value, the replacement cost prorated to remaining life. A furnace with two or three years left on a 20-year lifespan has used 85% to 90% of its value, so the credit is typically $300 to $800 on a standard furnace. That’s harder for a seller to reject because it’s accurate. I use the same logic on roofs, water heaters and AC units.

3. The house being a house: leave it off the request

  • Cosmetic items you could see at the showing: paint, outdated fixtures, old carpet
  • Cosmetic plaster cracks and minor grading issues
  • Older but working HVAC and outdated but working electrical
  • Deferred exterior maintenance and age-appropriate wear

Every cosmetic item you add gives the seller a reason to counter or reject the whole request. I’ve seen deals fall apart over a $200 faucet that was in the listing photos. Save the goodwill for the items that matter.

Which remedy to ask for

  • A credit, almost always. You control who does the work and when, and you can verify the quality. A repair done under seller pressure before closing is the minimum the seller could get away with. A credit can’t exceed your actual closing costs, and your lender has to approve it. On a conventional loan with 5% down, seller credits are typically capped at 3% of the price; FHA allows 6%; VA allows up to 4% plus reasonable closing costs.
  • A seller repair, when it’s required. An FHA or VA appraiser can flag items such as missing handrails, peeling paint on pre-1978 homes, broken windows, non-working HVAC and electrical hazards as required repairs, and the seller has to address them before the loan can close. Municipal occupancy inspections can require repairs too. A credit doesn’t satisfy either.
  • A price reduction, when a credit won’t fit. If you’re at the concession cap, your closing costs are already covered, or the appraisal is at the edge of the price, a lower price is the way to capture the same value.

When to hold firm, and when to take the counter

Hold firm on go/no-go items and lender-required repairs. A seller who rejects a targeted, well-documented request is betting the next buyer won’t find the same issues. They will. I’ve seen sellers lose $20,000 on a re-list because they walked away from a $4,000 repair request.

Take the counter when it covers your go/no-go items and most of your negotiating points, when what’s left is in the third list, or when the house is still right for you at that number. One rule to know before you reject a counter: in the Missouri contract, if you reject the seller’s counter, the seller can go back to your original request list. Get the list right the first time.

What Buyers Negotiate After Inspection

There’s no universal average, because two reports on two similar homes produce two different negotiating positions. In my experience, credits or price reductions after inspection typically run $2,000 to $8,000. A 22-year-old furnace that still runs is a different conversation from a Federal Pacific panel, active foundation movement and failed cast iron drains, which can justify much more or end the contract.

What decides the number is what the findings cost to fix and whether you have contractor estimates behind the ask. A documented $4,500 request is more likely to succeed than an undocumented $10,000 one.

How to Make the Ask and Have It Stick

  1. Get contractor estimates first for the two or three biggest findings: an HVAC company, a plumber, an electrician. Written estimates usually take a day or two.
  2. Focus on high-cost, documented items. Reference the inspection report pages and attach the quotes.
  3. Pick the right remedy: a credit, a price reduction or a repair, based on the item, your loan and your cash. How to choose ↑
  4. Anchor slightly above your target, because sellers usually counter.
  5. Know your walk-away number before you send it.

Your inspection deadline is fixed in the contract, Day 10 in the standard Missouri residential contract. Deliver the written notice before it.

When Sellers Say No

The seller counters lower: the most common outcome. Decide whether the counter covers your critical items; you can accept, counter again or walk. The seller rejects outright: rare, usually on as-is listings or when there’s a backup offer. Within your inspection period, you can terminate and get your earnest money back. The seller accepts: an addendum amends the contract, and any credit shows up on the Closing Disclosure.

Your strongest leverage is being willing to walk.

When to Walk vs. Negotiate

Not every repair finding is a negotiation. Some are a reason to terminate. Walk away when:

  • The cost of repairs exceeds the margin between the offer price and the home's repaired value. If you are already paying market value and the home needs $40,000 in structural repairs, there is no equity to be made at this price.
  • The seller refuses any negotiation on material findings and the home is not uniquely irreplaceable. A seller who will not move at all on a $12,000 roof is telling you something about what the rest of the transaction will look like.
  • The inspection reveals systemic issues -- horizontal foundation cracks, major structural settlement, evidence of long-term hidden water damage -- that suggest the repair estimates are floors, not ceilings.

Your inspection period exists precisely for this decision. Use it. The earnest money you lose by walking from a bad deal is almost always less than what you lose proceeding with the wrong property at the wrong price.

A Worked Example: South County Brick Ranch

Home listed at $245,000. During the showing I noted: HVAC data plate showed 2008 (17 years old), visible efflorescence in the northwest corner of the basement, gutters pulling away from the fascia on the rear of the home, and a Federal Pacific panel in the utility room.

My approach: offer at $234,000 -- reflecting a $7,000 discount for the likely near-term HVAC replacement and a $4,000 discount for the Federal Pacific panel -- while explicitly noting in the offer that we would be conducting a sewer scope. This sets up a legitimate post-inspection negotiation on the basement water issue rather than trying to price in something we cannot yet quantify.

Outcome: accepted at $234,000. Sewer scope showed root intrusion in the lateral. We submitted an inspection objection for $5,500 (midpoint of the scope-confirmed repair estimate). Seller accepted. Final effective price: $228,500 on a $245,000 listing -- $16,500 reduction supported by documented conditions at every step.

The key: every reduction was tied to a specific, documented condition with a cost estimate. Nothing was arbitrary. Sellers can reject a vague "we want $15,000 off" -- they have a much harder time rejecting three line items with contractor estimates attached.

More Questions About Inspection Negotiations

Should I buy a house with…

Should I buy a house with foundation cracks?

It depends on the crack. Horizontal or stair-step cracks are the ones to take seriously. In 2026 expected costs, minor foundation repair (crack injection, pier stabilization) runs $3,000 to $8,000, and major repair for horizontal cracks or significant movement runs $15,000 to $40,000 or more. Get a structural engineer’s evaluation before your inspection deadline, then decide between a credit, a price reduction or walking. 2026 expected repair costs →

Should I buy a house with a wet basement?

Often, if you price it right. Find out where the water is coming from before you decide what it’s worth. In 2026 expected costs, interior waterproofing with a drain and sump pump runs $5,000 to $12,000, and exterior excavation runs $15,000 to $25,000 or more. 2026 expected repair costs →

Should I buy a house with a sewer lateral problem?

It can still be a good buy if the problem is priced. A sewer scope, which belongs on every St. Louis home, runs $150 to $300. Replacement typically runs $4,000 to $12,000 and can exceed $15,000 in some cases. Credits beat repairs almost every time: back your ask with a contractor estimate. Some municipalities run sewer lateral programs, so check the area page. Credit, price reduction or repair →

Should I buy a house with a Federal Pacific electrical panel?

Yes, if you plan on replacing it. Federal Pacific and Zinsco panels need replacement, which runs $2,000 to $4,000 in 2026 expected costs. The panel can also affect insurance and VA financing. Federal Pacific panels and VA loans →

Making the ask

How do I get repair estimates before submitting an inspection objection?

Contact 2 to 3 licensed St. Louis contractors for the specific items flagged in your inspection report. Many will provide estimates based on the inspection report and photos without visiting the property -- especially for straightforward items like HVAC, roofing, and electrical panels. Your buyer agent should have a list of reliable contractors for this purpose.

Which inspection findings are worth negotiating in St. Louis?

Sort them into three lists. Go/no-go items, like Federal Pacific or Zinsco panels, active foundation movement, active sewer failure or a roof at the true end of its life, decide whether the deal works. Negotiating points, like an older roof or HVAC near the end of its life, usually become a credit prorated to remaining life. Cosmetic items and age-appropriate wear stay off the request. Take the counter when it covers those and most of your negotiating points, or when what’s left is cosmetic. A little. If your documented cost is $7,200, asking for about $8,500 leaves room for the counter most sellers make. Decide your walk-away number before you send it.

Deadlines and walking away

How long do I have to respond after a home inspection in Missouri?

In the Missouri residential contract, your inspection notice is due in writing by Day 10: you can be satisfied, terminate or negotiate, and the choice can’t be undone. If you negotiate, you have until Day 20 to reach written agreement, or the contract terminates and the earnest money is returned. Check the dates in your own contract. The inspection, step by step →

When should I walk away instead of negotiating?

When repairs exceed the margin between your price and the home’s repaired value, when the seller won’t move at all on material findings, or when the inspection shows systemic problems like horizontal foundation cracks or long-term hidden water damage, where estimates are floors, not ceilings. What doesn’t work in an inspection negotiation?

A laundry-list objection, objecting to cosmetic items buyers saw at the showing, asking without documentation (“the inspector said the HVAC is old” isn’t a position; a contractor estimate is) and missing the deadline.
