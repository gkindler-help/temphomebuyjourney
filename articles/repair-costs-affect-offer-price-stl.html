St. Louis Home Repair Cost Guide

How Repair Costs Should Affect Your Offer Price in St. Louis

Finding deferred maintenance during a showing is only half the work. The other half is knowing how to translate what you find into a number that makes sense -- and how to defend that number in a negotiation.

George Kindler· Licensed Missouri Realtor· For Buyers

The Framework

Repair costs affect your offer in one of two ways. Either you price them into your initial offer -- reducing what you are willing to pay by the estimated repair cost -- or you offer at list and negotiate a repair credit or price reduction after the inspection. Which approach makes more sense depends on the market conditions for that specific home.

Pricing In vs. Asking for a Credit

When to price repairs into your initial offer

In a balanced or buyer-favorable market, where the home has been sitting for 30 or more days or where similar homes are available, pricing known repair needs into your initial offer is the cleaner approach. You offer less than list, cite the specific items and estimated costs as your basis, and the seller can accept, counter, or reject on a fully informed basis.

This approach also protects you from the scenario where you offer at list, get under contract, and then discover in inspection that the roof needs immediate replacement -- at which point you are negotiating from a position of emotional attachment and sunk costs.

When to ask for a credit after inspection

In a competitive market where multiple offers are likely, pricing repair costs into an initial offer may cost you the home entirely. In this scenario, offering at or near list and reserving the right to negotiate after inspection allows you to compete while still protecting yourself from unknown repair costs.

The risk is that you may have less leverage after inspection than you anticipated. Sellers who receive multiple offers know their position.

The Rule of Thumb For visible, pre-inspection repair items -- roof age, HVAC age, obvious exterior deferred maintenance -- price them into your offer. For items that require inspection to confirm scope and cost -- sewer lateral condition, electrical system specifics, structural issues -- reserve those for post-inspection negotiation.

The Math

If a home is listed at $265,000 and your walkthrough reveals an HVAC system that is 18 years old (replacement cost: $8,000 to $12,000) and tuckpointing that needs attention on two sides of the exterior ($3,000 to $5,000), you have an identifiable repair liability of $11,000 to $17,000.

An offer of $250,000 -- $15,000 below list -- is not a lowball offer in this context. It is a market-rate offer that prices the known condition accurately. The negotiation letter explains exactly what you found and what it will cost to address. That is a defensible position.

When the Seller Will Not Negotiate

Some sellers -- particularly estate sales, corporate-owned properties, and sellers who received multiple offers -- will not negotiate on condition. Their position is that the price reflects the condition and you can take it or leave it.

In this scenario you have three choices: accept and buy knowing what it will cost you, counter and lose the home to another buyer, or walk away. None of these is automatically wrong. The question is whether the home at list price, including repair costs, is still a reasonable transaction for you. Sometimes it is. Sometimes it is not.

This is why knowing your numbers before you make an offer -- not after you are emotionally invested -- matters so much.
