Price Reduction After Inspection in St. Louis: What to Ask For

Last updated November 22, 2026

The inspection found problems. Now what should you ask the seller for? Start with the repair costs, separate actual failures from ordinary wear, and build a request you can support with evidence.

George Kindler · Licensed Missouri Realtor · For Buyers

How much can you negotiate after inspection?

It depends on what the inspection found, not the home’s age or price. In my experience, credits or reductions after inspection typically run $2,000 to $8,000. Major system or structural failures, such as a roof, foundation or sewer lateral, can justify more when you have contractor estimates to back it up.

Step 2

Here's what's next

You have the inspection findings and a proposed number. Now you need to support the request, decide what you want the seller to do, and respond before your deadline.

  1. Get contractor estimates. Focus on the two or three biggest findings first: an HVAC company, a plumber, an electrician, or the specialist the inspection calls for. Written estimates usually take a day or two.

    Reference the inspection report pages and attach the quotes. A documented $4,500 request is more likely to succeed than an undocumented $10,000 one.

    When you can't get a final bid in time, don't pick a number out of the air. Build it from more than one source: your inspector's estimate, published install costs, and quotes contractors have given on similar work. Ask for a number that sits inside all of them, and remember you're also taking on whatever isn't known yet: design, permits, and scope that could grow.

    On a lake cabin with a failing septic, the inspector said $18,000 to $24,000, published installs ran about the same, and contractors I've worked with had quoted $20,000 to $25,000. We asked for $20,000 without a final bid. The final cost came in around $16,000 after closing. That was the actual repair cost, not the negotiated amount. Where the $20,000 came from →

  2. Pick the remedy for each item. A credit, a completed repair, and a price reduction are not interchangeable. Match the remedy to the problem, your loan, and your available cash.
    Which remedy makes sense?

    A credit, almost always. You control who does the work and when, and you can verify the quality. A repair done under seller pressure before closing may be the minimum the seller could get away with.

    A credit can't exceed your actual eligible closing costs, and your lender has to approve it. On a conventional loan with 5% down, seller credits are typically capped at 3% of the price.

    A seller repair, when it's required. An FHA or VA appraiser can flag items such as missing handrails, peeling paint on pre-1978 homes, broken windows, non-working HVAC and electrical hazards as required repairs. Municipal occupancy inspections can require repairs too. A credit doesn't satisfy either requirement.

    For major items affecting your loan, occupancy, or ability to move in without worry, I want the repair completed before closing. A credit doesn't fix the problem; it leaves you responsible for making the repair.

    A price reduction, when a credit won't fit. If you're at the concession cap, your closing costs are already covered, or the appraisal is at the edge of the price, a lower purchase price can capture value that a credit cannot.

    For smaller items you can live with for a while, a credit or home warranty may make more sense than a handyman visit. A dated system that still works, like an older furnace, has no repair to make. A home warranty may be a more realistic request.

    The worksheet's comparison in Step D shows how a credit, price reduction, seller repairs, or no agreement affects your numbers.

  3. Know your walk-away number. Before you send the request, decide what you can still afford to repair yourself and which problems must be resolved for the purchase to work.

    Hold firm on go/no-go items and lender-required repairs. Take a counter when it covers those items and most of your negotiating points, when what's left is cosmetic, or when the house is still right for you at that number.

    A seller who rejects a targeted, well-documented request is betting the next buyer won't find the same issues. They will. I've seen sellers lose $20,000 on a re-list because they walked away from a $4,000 repair request.

    Some findings are a reason to terminate rather than negotiate. That includes repairs that exceed the margin between your purchase price and the home's repaired value, a seller refusing to address material findings, or systemic damage where the estimate may be a floor rather than a ceiling.

    If you're already paying market value and the house needs $40,000 in structural repairs, there may be no equity to be made at that price. Likewise, a seller who won't move on a $12,000 roof is telling you something about the rest of the transaction.

    Major structural settlement, horizontal foundation cracks, and long-term hidden water damage deserve particular care. Your inspection period exists precisely for this decision. Use it.

  4. Deliver the written notice before Day 10. Under the standard Missouri residential contract discussed here, the inspection deadline is Day 10.

    Focus on the high-cost, documented findings. Reference the relevant inspection pages, attach contractor estimates, and state the requested remedy. Anchor slightly above your target when appropriate, because sellers usually counter.

    Deliver your inspection notice in writing before the deadline. Your own contract's options and dates control, including the notice requirements, response periods, and any right to terminate. Do not rely on a general timeline in place of your signed agreement.

What the seller can say

Once the written request is delivered, the seller can accept it, make a counteroffer, or reject it. Your next move depends on that response and the options in your contract.

What did the seller say?

Get it in a signed addendum, and make sure a credit or price change matches what your lender sees on the Closing Disclosure. If it's a credit, check it fits under your loan's limit.

Check whether the counter covers your go/no-go items and most of your negotiating points. Put the seller's number into step E of the worksheet to see what you'd still be paying for. Before you reject a counter, remember that in the Missouri contract the seller can go back to your original request list.

Within your inspection period, decide whether to proceed as is or end the contract under the options it gives you. A seller who won't move at all on a material, documented finding is telling you something about the rest of the deal.

Spell out the scope, who does the work, and the proof you'll get: receipts, lien waivers, and a reinspection or a look at the final walk-through. You inherit the quality of the work.

The seller counters lower: the most common outcome. Decide whether the counter covers your critical items. You can accept, counter again, or walk if your contract allows it.

The seller rejects outright: rare, usually on as-is listings or when there's a backup offer. Within your inspection period, the standard Missouri contract can let you terminate and receive your earnest money back. Check the options and requirements in your own contract.

The seller accepts: a signed addendum amends the contract, and an approved seller credit is reflected on the Closing Disclosure.

The seller offers to make repairs: define the work in writing, identify who will perform it, and verify completion before closing. Receipts, lien waivers where applicable, and a reinspection or final walk-through help establish what was actually completed.

One rule before rejecting a counter In the Missouri contract, rejecting the seller's counter can allow the seller to return to your original request list. Get that list right the first time, and review the provisions in your own agreement.

Your strongest leverage is being willing to walk. But that decision has to make sense for the actual property, the documented findings, and the cost you'll be responsible for after closing.

Step 4

Here's why it's important

A repair estimate is not automatically a price reduction. The number has to make sense, the finding has to support the request, and you need to know which items are actually worth negotiating.

The Midpoint Rule

Get realistic cost estimates from contractors. Once you have a range, use the midpoint as your adjustment basis.

The Midpoint Rule Use the midpoint of the repair cost range as your adjustment basis. Asking for the top of the range looks like cherry-picking; the midpoint is defensible and harder for a seller to reject without appearing unreasonable.

For example, a roof with an expected repair range of $8,000 to $20,000 has a midpoint of $14,000. That gives you a defensible starting point for a proposed adjustment, subject to the actual condition and the contractor's estimate.

Condition matters more than age

An older system is not the same thing as a failed system. A furnace can be past its expected service life and still be working. That alone does not create the same negotiating position as a confirmed failure.

A failing system is different from a dated one. When the inspection on a Fenton ranch found a furnace that was failing, we asked for the repair before closing and a home warranty, because a warranty doesn't cover a problem the inspector already found. Why we asked for both →

The same distinction applies to other findings. An old plaster crack is not necessarily active foundation movement. A dated roof is not necessarily a failed roof. The condition, supporting evidence, and cost of correcting the problem are what matter.

A targeted request beats a laundry list

I go through every report with my clients before we respond, and we sort it into three lists. Most buyers see the full report and want to ask for everything. That's the wrong move.

A targeted request is more likely to succeed and less likely to blow up the deal.

1. Go/no-go items: hold firm or walk

These aren't ordinary negotiating points. They decide whether the deal works. Any one of them can kill a financed deal if it isn't addressed before closing.

  • Federal Pacific or Zinsco electrical panels
  • Active foundation movement (moving cracks, not old ones) and horizontal cracks in block walls
  • Evidence of active sewer failure
  • A roof at the true end of its life with no remaining coverage
  • Knob-and-tube or aluminum branch wiring on an FHA or VA loan
  • Active water intrusion, structural problems, and anything that would keep you from getting a mortgage or homeowners insurance

Remedy: a repair completed before closing. A credit doesn't fix a problem like this, and these are the items that affect your loan, occupancy and moving in without worry.

If the seller won't address them, walking away is a business decision, not a failure.

2. Negotiating points: match the remedy to the item

  • An older roof with limited remaining life
  • An HVAC system or water heater at the end of its service range
  • An active termite infestation (old treated damage with a clear current report is informational)
  • Safety items such as missing handrails, exposed wiring, missing GFCI outlets near water, and smoke or CO detectors

Match the remedy to the item. Safety items and active problems, like an active termite infestation, get a repair completed before closing; on FHA and VA loans many of them are required.

Small items you can live with for a while can be a credit or a home warranty instead of a handyman visit.

A system that's old but still works, like a dated furnace, has no repair to make. You usually can't get a repair or a credit for it, but you can likely get a home warranty.

3. The house being a house: leave it off the request

  • Cosmetic items you could see at the showing: paint, outdated fixtures, old carpet
  • Cosmetic plaster cracks and minor grading issues
  • Older but working HVAC and outdated but working electrical
  • Deferred exterior maintenance and age-appropriate wear

Every cosmetic item you add gives the seller a reason to counter or reject the whole request.

I've seen deals fall apart over a $200 faucet that was in the listing photos. Save the goodwill for the items that matter.

Documentation makes the request defensible

The inspection report identifies the problem. The contractor estimate puts a cost on correcting it.

Together, they give the seller a specific finding and a supported amount to respond to, rather than an arbitrary request for money off the purchase price.

Focus on the inspection pages and contractor estimates that support the actual request. A long list of minor findings does not make the expensive repairs more convincing.

A Worked Example: South County Brick Ranch

Home listed at $245,000. During the showing I noted: HVAC data plate showed 2008 (17 years old), visible efflorescence in the northwest corner of the basement, gutters pulling away from the fascia on the rear of the home, and a Federal Pacific panel in the utility room.

My approach: offer at $234,000 -- reflecting a $7,000 discount for the likely near-term HVAC replacement and a $4,000 discount for the Federal Pacific panel -- while explicitly noting in the offer that we would be conducting a sewer scope.

This sets up a legitimate post-inspection negotiation on the basement water issue rather than trying to price in something we cannot yet quantify.

Outcome: accepted at $234,000. Sewer scope showed root intrusion in the lateral. We submitted an inspection objection for $5,500 (midpoint of the scope-confirmed repair estimate). Seller accepted.

The key: every reduction was tied to a specific, documented condition with a cost estimate. Nothing was arbitrary.

Sellers can reject a vague "we want $15,000 off" -- they have a much harder time rejecting three line items with contractor estimates attached.

More Questions About Inspection Negotiations

Additional answers about common inspection findings, negotiating requests, and Missouri contract deadlines. Expand the questions that apply to your situation.

Should I buy a house with foundation cracks?

It depends on the crack. Horizontal or stair-step cracks are the ones to take seriously. In 2026 expected costs, minor foundation repair (crack injection, pier stabilization) runs $3,000 to $8,000, and major repair for horizontal cracks or significant movement runs $15,000 to $40,000 or more.

Get a structural engineer's evaluation before your inspection deadline, then ask for the repair completed before closing or walk. On a major item like this, a credit doesn't fix the problem.

2026 expected repair costs → Flag it in the walk-through tool →

Should I buy a house with a wet basement?

Often, if the cause gets fixed. Find out where the water is coming from first. Active water intrusion is a major item, so ask for the repair completed before closing.

In 2026 expected costs, interior waterproofing with a drain and sump pump runs $5,000 to $12,000, and exterior excavation runs $15,000 to $25,000 or more.

2026 expected repair costs → Flag it in the walk-through tool →

Should I buy a house with a sewer lateral problem?

It can still be a good buy if the problem is fixed before you own it. A sewer scope, which belongs on every St. Louis home, runs $150 to $300.

Replacement typically runs $4,000 to $12,000 and can exceed $15,000 in some cases. A failed lateral is a major item, so ask for the repair completed before closing, backed by a contractor estimate.

Some municipalities run sewer lateral programs, so check the area page.

Credit, price reduction or repair → Flag it in the walk-through tool →

Should I buy a house with a Federal Pacific electrical panel?

Yes, if it's replaced. Federal Pacific and Zinsco panels need replacement, which runs $2,000 to $4,000 in 2026 expected costs.

Because the panel can affect insurance and VA financing, ask for the replacement completed before closing.

How do I get repair estimates before submitting an inspection objection?

Contact 2 to 3 licensed St. Louis contractors for the specific items flagged in your inspection report.

Many will provide estimates based on the inspection report and photos without visiting the property -- especially for straightforward items like HVAC, roofing, and electrical panels.

Which inspection findings are worth negotiating in St. Louis?

Sort them into three lists. Go/no-go items, like Federal Pacific or Zinsco panels, active foundation movement, active sewer failure or a roof at the true end of its life, decide whether the deal works.

Negotiating points get matched to the item: a repair completed before closing for safety items and active problems, a credit or home warranty for small items you can live with for a while, and usually a home warranty for dated systems that still work.

Cosmetic items and age-appropriate wear stay off the request. The three lists ↑

When should the seller make the repair instead of giving a credit?

For major items, nearly always: anything that can affect your loan, occupancy or moving in without worry.

An FHA or VA appraiser can flag items like missing handrails, peeling paint on pre-1978 homes, broken windows, non-working HVAC and electrical hazards as repairs that must be done before the loan can close, and municipal occupancy inspections can require repairs too.

A credit doesn't fix the issue; it makes you responsible for a repair that was on the seller. A credit or a home warranty makes more sense for small items you can live with temporarily.

Which remedy to ask for ↑

When should I hold firm on an inspection request, and when should I take the counter?

Hold firm on go/no-go items, such as Federal Pacific or Zinsco panels, active foundation movement, active sewer failure or a roof at the end of its life, and on repairs your lender requires.

Take the counter when it covers those and most of your negotiating points, or when what's left is cosmetic.

If you reject a seller's counter in the Missouri contract, the seller can go back to your original list.

Seller responses and what happens next ↑

How much should I ask for above my repair estimate?

A little. If your documented cost is $7,200, asking for about $8,500 leaves room for the counter most sellers make. Decide your walk-away number before you send it.

How long do I have to respond after a home inspection in Missouri?

In the Missouri residential contract, your inspection notice is due in writing by Day 10: you can be satisfied, terminate or negotiate, and the choice can't be undone.

If you negotiate, you have until Day 20 to reach written agreement, or the contract terminates and the earnest money is returned.

Your own contract's options and dates control. Check the deadlines and requirements in your signed agreement.

When should I walk away instead of negotiating?

When repairs exceed the margin between your price and the home's repaired value, when the seller won't move at all on material findings, or when the inspection shows systemic problems like horizontal foundation cracks or long-term hidden water damage, where estimates are floors, not ceilings.

What doesn't work in an inspection negotiation?

A laundry-list objection, objecting to cosmetic items buyers saw at the showing, asking without documentation ("the inspector said the HVAC is old" isn't a position; a contractor estimate is) and missing the deadline.
