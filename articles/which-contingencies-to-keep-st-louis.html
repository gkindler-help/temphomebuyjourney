Which Contingencies Should I Keep When Buying in St. Louis?

Last updated October 21, 2026

Inspection, financing, appraisal and sometimes the sale of your own home: each contingency is a way out of the contract that keeps your earnest money. Here’s what each one protects, when giving one up actually helps, and the middle-ground options I use instead of a full waiver.

George Kindler· Licensed Missouri Realtor· Buyers

Contingencies in St. Louis, Answered

Which contingencies should I keep when buying in St. Louis?

For most financed buyers, all three: the inspection, the financing and the appraisal. Each one is a way out of the contract that keeps your earnest money, so giving one up is a decision about risk you can afford, not a default way to win. A modified version usually beats a full waiver. What each one protects ↓

Should I waive the inspection?

It can work. I’ve had buyers waive the inspection, and in this market I don’t think one is a requirement. But it takes an agent who can read a house’s condition, because the risk is what’s beneath the hood: the sewer lateral, radon and termites. I usually prefer a modified version. The middle-ground options ↓

Should I waive the financing contingency?

Very rarely. I’ve done it only for very strong borrowers whose file an underwriter had already reviewed and who understood their earnest money was at risk, usually on very clean houses. Underwriting approves the buyer and then the property, and an approved buyer doesn’t guarantee the house will qualify. Why it’s riskier than it looks ↓

Should I waive the appraisal contingency?

Only if you have the cash to cover a gap. If the house appraises low, your lender lends on the appraised value, so you either pay the difference out of pocket or walk away from your earnest money. The same rule applies to an appraisal gap guarantee. What a low appraisal costs →

Do I get my earnest money back if I back out?

Usually, if you back out under a contingency before its deadline. Most buyers who walk away do it during the inspection period and get it back. Back out after your contingencies end, or miss a deadline, and the seller can ask to keep it. Earnest money at each deadline ↓

What does each contingency protect?

Each one lets you leave the contract with your earnest money if a specific thing goes wrong before its deadline. Your contract sets every date.

ContingencyIt protects you ifWaiving it means
InspectionYou find a condition you can’t live with or can’t negotiateYou buy the house as it is, including what you can’t see
FinancingYour lender doesn’t issue a written loan commitmentIf the loan falls through, your earnest money is at risk
Appraisal (a rider on the St. Louis contract)The house appraises below your priceYou cover the gap in cash or lose your earnest money
Sale of your homeYour current house doesn’t sell in timeYou may carry two payments

The deadlines are where contingencies are won or lost. The inspection period is commonly about 10 days on a St. Louis contract, and the loan and appraisal dates run later. Let a deadline pass and that protection can end on its own. Your deadlines after an accepted offer →

What are the middle-ground options to waiving the inspection?

You can give up most of the inspection’s leverage without going in blind. These are the options I use most:

  • Keep Option 2, give up Option 3. The contract’s inspection notice gives you three choices: accept the house and move forward (Option 1), terminate without cause (Option 2), or ask for repairs through an inspection resolution (Option 3). Giving up Option 3 keeps your right to walk away during the inspection period, providing the inspection reports if the seller asks, but the seller knows there won’t be a second negotiation over repairs.
  • Inspect only specific items. You inspect named items, such as the roof, the furnace or the sewer lateral, and those items must be in serviceable condition.
  • Set a dollar floor. You agree not to ask for any single item under $250, so the seller isn’t facing a list of small maintenance items.
  • Shorten the window. Book the inspector and the sewer scope before you write, and give yourself fewer days to decide.

To a seller, the second negotiation is what these remove. On a Wintergreen Estates listing I sold in Fenton, the $406,000 buyers kept every contingency, but their agent told me they wouldn’t ask for any repairs. I went on instinct about that agent and on knowing the house wouldn’t present any issues, and treated the offer as about as strong as $410,000 with a negotiation still ahead. A verbal promise isn’t binding. Giving up Option 3 puts it in writing. How that sale played out →

What am I risking if I skip the inspection?

The conditions you can’t see at a showing. In St. Louis that’s mainly the sewer lateral, radon and termites, plus whatever an older house hides behind finished walls. In 2,872 St. Louis County sales in 2026, 74.8% of the homes were built before 1980.

Why is waiving the financing contingency riskier than it looks?

Because underwriting is two-fold: the lender approves you, and then it approves the property. A buyer whose file an underwriter has already reviewed has cleared the first half. The house still has to meet the loan’s property requirements and appraise, and if it doesn’t, the loan can fall through with your earnest money on the line.

So the agent has to know conforming property requirements well enough to spot a house that may not qualify, such as one with condition problems a lender will want fixed before it funds. It’s very rare for me to advise waiving financing. When we’ve done it, the buyers were very strong borrowers, an underwriter had already reviewed their file, they understood their earnest money was at risk, and the house was very clean.

When does giving up a contingency actually win the house?

When it removes a risk the seller is weighing against a higher offer. Sellers compare what each offer nets and how likely it is to close, so a waived or limited contingency can be worth a few thousand dollars of price. It doesn’t help when there’s no competition: on a house that’s been sitting, keep your protections and negotiate. What days on market tells you →

Strong terms and protection can go together. On a lake cabin in Ste. Genevieve County, my parents won with list price, cash and a quick close, but kept their inspection protections. The septic system became the whole negotiation. How the septic negotiation played out →

What happens to my earnest money at each deadline?

It’s safest while your contingencies are open and most at risk after they close. About 1% of the price is typical for a financed buyer in St. Louis, and that’s the money a waived contingency puts on the line.

  • During the inspection period: you can terminate under the contract’s terms and get it back.
  • After inspections, before closing: your remaining protection is the loan and appraisal contingencies, if you kept them. Walking away for another reason puts the deposit at risk.
  • If you miss a deadline or don’t perform on the contract: the seller can ask to keep it. When buyers have failed to meet their contingencies, my sellers have kept it.

Either way, it moves by mutual release, a form both sides sign. If they don’t agree, Missouri law requires the broker holding it to turn the disputed funds over to the State Treasurer’s Unclaimed Property Division within 365 days of the projected closing date (RSMo 339.105.4).

More Questions About Contingencies

Your loan and your house

Do FHA and VA loans change what I can waive?

Partly. FHA and VA hold the property to a higher safety standard than conventional loans, so the house still has to meet those requirements even if you skip the inspection. Both also require a clause that lets you out of the contract without losing your earnest money if the appraisal comes in below the price. VA buyers usually have more room to get a loan commitment than FHA buyers. FHA vs. conventional →

Does a home-sale contingency weaken my offer?

Usually. A seller who takes one is buying the risk that your house won’t sell, so you’ll typically pay list or more and have less leverage. First see whether you can get approved without it. Buying with a home-sale contingency →

Do cash buyers need contingencies?

Fewer. A cash offer has no loan or lender appraisal to protect, so it usually comes down to the inspection, which still matters. On cash deals I’ve always recommended 10% earnest money. How much earnest money to offer →

Inspections

Does waiving the inspection skip the city’s occupancy inspection?

No. Many St. Louis County municipalities require their own occupancy or point-of-sale inspection when a house changes hands. That’s set by the city, not your contract, and what it finds still has to be handled. Why the address matters →
