Fixer Upper vs. Move-In Ready in St. Louis:
The Real Math by Neighborhood

Last updated October 21, 2026

A fixer-upper’s price leaves out the work. Once you add the renovation, the financing and the time, the cheaper house isn’t always cheaper. Here’s how to compare the two paths on total cost.

Fixer-Upper vs. Move-In Ready, Answered

Should I buy a fixer-upper or a move-in-ready home?

Compare total cost, not list prices. Add a contractor’s number to the fixer-upper’s price, then compare it with move-in ready. If both land at the same cost in the same area, move-in ready usually wins. A fixer-upper makes sense when it gets you a house you couldn’t otherwise afford and you have a verified scope, reserves and a flexible timeline. When a fixer-upper makes sense ↓

What does “move-in ready” mean in St. Louis?

Structural first, cosmetic second. A working HVAC system, a roof with life left, no active basement water, no major electrical red flags or foundation movement, and a house likely to get through financing and appraisal without drama. It doesn’t mean luxury finishes; a clean 1998 ranch with older countertops but solid systems can qualify. What move-in ready means ↓

In the calculator’s 2025–2026 MARIS sales, move-in-ready midpoints ran from about $219,000 in the 63125 Lemay corridor to about $600,000 in Kirkwood (63122) and $800,000 in Chesterfield (63005). How much more does move-in ready cost than a fixer-upper?

It depends on the area. In South County, the gap between as-is and move-in-ready sales ran about $50,000 to $130,000 depending on the ZIP code. In St. Charles County, with newer homes, it’s narrower, closer to $25,000 to $40,000. Sometimes not. A $250,000 house that needs a roof, HVAC, flooring and basement waterproofing can cost more than a $310,000 house that needs almost nothing, before you count contractor delays and financing complications. 2026 expected repair costs →

Why do fixer-uppers look so cheap?

Because the price leaves out the work. In the calculator’s 2025–2026 data, as-is homes in Oakville’s 63129 ZIP code sold around $311,000, against about $360,000 for move-in ready. In Kirkwood (63122) it was about $400,000 as-is against $600,000 move-in ready.

That spread attracts buyers. And it misleads them.

The fixer upper price is what you pay at closing. What you spend after closing — kitchen, bathrooms, roof, HVAC, electrical — is not in that number. A buyer who sees $311,000 and thinks "I'm getting a deal" may be right. Or they may be buying a $311,000 house that needs $90,000 of work, putting them at $401,000 total — above the move-in ready price in the same zip code, in a home they cannot fully live in for months.

In St. Louis the condition gap is wider than many buyers expect because of the age of the housing. Much of the city and inner-ring suburbs is older brick construction that holds up structurally but carries aging electrical panels, older plumbing and deferred maintenance that shows up fast in an inspection report.

The real question The fixer upper comparison is not purchase price vs. purchase price. It is total cost of ownership on both paths — and which neighborhoods become available at that same total cost. That is what this article and the comparison tool are built to show.

What does it cost to renovate a house in St. Louis?

As 2026 expected costs, a general refresh runs about $15,000 to $25,000, a moderate renovation $35,000 to $60,000 and a full renovation $75,000 to $120,000 or more, before any structural work. They’re a place to start a budget; you still need contractor quotes. St. Louis housing adds its own line items: Federal Pacific panels, installed in millions of homes between the 1950s and 1980s; clay tile sewer laterals, standard in St. Louis homes built before 1980; and tuckpointing on older brick.

Before choosing between paths, make sure you understand the hidden risks that come with lower-priced homes. What buyers regret most after buying a cheap house in St. Louis covers the infrastructure issues — sewer laterals, basement moisture, cosmetic flips — that catch buyers off guard after closing.

How does this data define a move-in-ready sale?

As a home that sold in under 14 days at or above its list price. By that measure, the calculator’s 2025–2026 sales clustered in three tiers: about $250,000 to $350,000 for entry-level ranches and updated older homes, $350,000 to $550,000 in the mid-range, and $600,000 and up where condition and location premiums stack. By ZIP code, midpoints ran from about $219,000 in 63125 to about $600,000 in Kirkwood (63122) and $800,000 in Chesterfield (63005).

The term gets abused online. To most St. Louis buyers, move-in ready means a working HVAC system, a roof with real life left, updated or well-maintained kitchen and baths, no active basement water, no major electrical red flags, no serious foundation movement, and a house likely to pass financing and appraisal without drama. It doesn’t mean luxury finishes or a full remodel. In St. Louis, move-in ready is structural first, cosmetic second.

The highest density of fixer upper opportunity in the metro. How does your loan change the fixer-upper math?

It changes what you can shop, not just your payment. Paying for the work from savings keeps the loan smaller; a renovation loan rolls the work into the loan at a higher cost. A buyer at $80,000 income shopping for a fixer upper with $45,000 in planned renovation will land in different zip codes depending on whether they use cash reserves, a HomeStyle loan, or FHA 203k — because each path changes the effective purchase price that gets matched against the market.

Path 1 — Conventional + Cash Reserves

You borrow against the purchase price only. Renovation is paid from savings after closing. Monthly payment is lower because you are borrowing less. On a $240,000 fixer upper with $45,000 renovation paid out of pocket, your mortgage is $240,000. Total out is $285,000 — which matches the move-in ready budget at this income level. The key difference: reserves are $45,000 lighter after closing, and if renovation scope grows, you are drawing from whatever remains.

This path works best for buyers with strong savings, realistic renovation scope, and no hard timeline. It is the lowest-risk financing approach if the reserves can support it.

Path 2 — HomeStyle Renovation Loan (Fannie Mae)

Purchase price plus renovation budget rolled into one conventional loan. One closing. The rate is often higher than a standard conventional loan, so get both quotes; on a $285,000 loan, each extra percentage point is roughly $195 more per month at 7% to 8%. As little as 3% down on a one-unit primary home. Reserves stay intact. The tradeoff is higher monthly payment and more underwriting complexity — the lender reviews renovation scope and requires contractor approval before closing.

Path 3 — FHA 203(k) Limited (formerly Streamlined)

Caps renovation at $75,000 (HUD raised it from $35,000 in November 2024) and limits work to non-structural repairs — no structural changes, no foundation work, no room additions. Minimum 3.5% down with a 580+ credit score under FHA rules; lenders often require more. Expect a longer closing than a standard FHA loan. FHA mortgage insurance adds 1.75% upfront (rolled into the loan) plus 0.55% annually — on a $260,000 loan that is approximately $119 per month that lasts the life of the loan unless you refinance into conventional.

The Limited version works for a house that needs paint, floors, a kitchen refresh or system replacements that fit under the cap. It won’t work if the house needs structural repair.

Path 4 — FHA 203(k) Standard

No separate renovation cap beyond FHA loan limits. Structural work allowed. Same down payment and credit requirements as the Limited loan, but a HUD 203(k) consultant is required. Loan proceeds are managed by the lender and distributed in phases. The Standard version is the right tool for a property that needs a roof, foundation work, or full systems replacement — but it adds process complexity. Expect 60 or more days to close and a draw schedule that governs when contractors get paid.

Not every St. Louis lender offers the 203k Standard. The 203k rehabilitation loan requires lenders who have experience with the draw process and HUD consultant requirements. Verify your lender has closed 203k loans in St. Louis before starting the process.

Does a fixer-upper change what you can shop?

It can, depending on the loan. In the calculator, a buyer at $80,000 income with $20,000 down at 7% has a budget of about $285,000. For context, 2026 median sale prices were $385,000 in Oakville, $323,500 in Mehlville, $305,000 in Arnold and $273,000 in Affton.

On the conventional-plus-savings path with a $45,000 moderate renovation, that buyer shops houses around $240,000 instead of $285,000. Same total cost, a different set of listings, and $45,000 less in reserves after closing.

With a HomeStyle loan, the loan covers the full $285,000, so the buyer shops the same price range as move-in ready while paying a higher rate to manage a renovation. On that path, the fixer-upper doesn’t open anything new.

What do buyers who do well with fixer-uppers have in common?

They knew the scope before they made an offer: not a guess, not a hope, but a contractor walkthrough with a number attached. Two hours and a few hundred dollars before the offer. That is the difference between a project and a money pit.

I am a Marine Corps veteran who became a real estate agent in St. Louis. I have closed VA loans on fixer uppers, watched buyers take on South City bungalows that turned into eight-month projects, and watched other buyers walk into Affton with a contractor and a clear scope and come out with exactly the house they wanted at a price that made sense.

The buyers who struggle almost always made an offer based on how the house felt. A brick bungalow on a nice street in Affton. They can see what it could be. That is a real instinct. But a failing sewer lateral, a Federal Pacific panel in the utility room and negative grading around the foundation do not care about the potential. They have a cost. That cost has to fit inside the budget before you close, not after.

The second thing I tell buyers: the math only works if the neighborhood shifts. If a HomeStyle loan at +1% puts you in the same zip code as a move-in ready purchase, you are paying more per month for the privilege of managing a renovation. That is a lifestyle choice, not a financial one. There is nothing wrong with it — but understand what you are choosing.

Can VA buyers buy a fixer-upper?

Sometimes, but the house has to pass the VA appraisal first. The appraiser checks VA minimum property requirements, and items like a failing roof, active water intrusion or a safety hazard usually have to be fixed before the loan funds. A Federal Pacific panel doesn’t automatically end a VA deal, but it can come up. VA renovation loans exist, but very few St. Louis lenders offer them, so the realistic options are negotiating repairs before closing, adjusting the price or walking away. When does a fixer-upper make sense?

When the total cost gets you a house you couldn’t afford move-in ready, a contractor has verified the scope, and you have the reserves and the time to finish the work.

When the Math Works

  • The total cost gets you a house you couldn’t buy move-in ready. If purchase price plus renovation lands at the same total as a move-in-ready home but gets you the house, street or size you actually want, that is a real advantage.
  • You have realistic scope verified by a contractor. Not an estimate from a YouTube video. A licensed contractor who walked the property, looked at the panel, checked the sewer clean-out, and gave you a number with contingency built in.
  • Your timeline is flexible. A renovation loan draw schedule means staged payments, possible temporary housing, and unexpected delays. Buyers on a hard timeline — job relocation, school enrollment deadline — should factor this before committing.
  • The property passed a pre-offer walkthrough. Two hours with a trusted contractor before you write changes everything you know going in.

When It Does Not Work

  • When the renovation loan rate premium erases the neighborhood advantage
  • When the fixer upper price already reflects the needed work — other buyers walked for a reason, and that reason is usually priced in
  • When the scope is unknown at offer time — discovering issues after inspection is how $40,000 in surprises happens
  • On a VA loan without pre-screening
  • When the FHA 203(k) Limited loan’s $75,000 cap doesn’t cover what the property actually needs
The buyers who regret it In 13 years of St. Louis transactions, the fixer-upper buyers who wished they had bought move-in ready almost always had one thing in common: they did not know the full scope before they closed.

More Questions About Move-In-Ready Homes

Move-in ready

Is a flipped house move-in ready?

Not necessarily. I’ve walked through beautifully staged flips with serious hidden issues behind the walls. Cosmetic updates don’t equal structural soundness, so the inspection matters just as much.

How fast do move-in-ready homes sell in St. Louis?

Fast when they’re priced right. In 2026 St. Louis sales, 990 homes sold within 4 days at a median 104% of list, while 390 sat more than 60 days. The well-priced, move-in-ready houses are the ones that draw multiple offers in a weekend. When the house has multiple offers →

Financing and condition

Why does a home’s condition matter to my lender?

Peeling paint, foundation concerns, roof problems or active water intrusion can complicate FHA and VA loans, insurance underwriting and appraisals, even if you’re willing to do the work. That’s a big reason move-in-ready homes sell at a premium. How your loan changes the math ↑

Can VA buyers buy move-in-ready homes in St. Louis?

Yes, and they’re often a better fit, because the VA appraisal has minimum property requirements. Deferred maintenance like roof issues, foundation concerns, peeling paint or water intrusion can fail a VA appraisal regardless of price. Can VA buyers buy a fixer-upper? ↑
