Fixer Upper vs. Move-In Ready in St. Louis:
The Real Math by Neighborhood

St. Louis has the largest price gap between fixer uppers and move-in ready homes of any major metro — 64% below on average. But the renovation costs, financing choice, and neighborhood shift change the comparison completely. Here's what your budget actually buys on both paths.

Buyer Guide · George Kindler · 13 Years · 250+ Transactions · Updated May 2026

Why St. Louis Fixer Uppers Look So Cheap

St. Louis fixer uppers average 64% below move-in ready homes — the largest spread of any major metro in the country. A move-in ready home in Oakville that sells for $360,000 has a fixer upper counterpart in the same zip code at $311,000. In Kirkwood the gap is even wider — $400,000 for a fixer upper versus $600,000 move-in ready.

That spread attracts buyers. And it misleads them.

The fixer upper price is what you pay at closing. What you spend after closing — kitchen, bathrooms, roof, HVAC, electrical — is not in that number. A buyer who sees $311,000 and thinks "I'm getting a deal" may be right. Or they may be buying a $311,000 house that needs $90,000 of work, putting them at $401,000 total — above the move-in ready price in the same zip code, in a home they can't live in for six months.

The real question The fixer upper comparison isn't purchase price vs. purchase price. It's total cost of ownership on both paths — and which neighborhoods become available at that same total cost.

How Financing Changes the Math

Most buyers buying a fixer upper think about the purchase price and the renovation budget as two separate numbers. They are — until you choose how to finance the renovation. That choice changes your monthly payment, your down payment requirement, your buying power, and in some cases which neighborhoods you can afford.

Path 1 — Conventional Loan + Cash Reserves

You borrow against the purchase price only. The renovation budget comes from savings after closing. Your monthly payment is lower because you're borrowing less. But your cash reserves take a hit — and if the renovation scope grows (it always can), you're drawing from a limited pool.

This path works best for buyers with strong savings, good credit, and realistic renovation scope. It's the lowest-risk financing approach if you have the reserves to support it.

Path 2 — HomeStyle Renovation Loan (Fannie Mae)

Purchase price plus renovation budget rolled into one conventional loan. One closing. Rate runs approximately 1% above a standard conventional rate — on a $300,000 loan that's about $175 more per month. No mortgage insurance if you put 5%+ down.

The advantage: reserves stay intact. The tradeoff: higher monthly payment and slightly more underwriting complexity — the lender reviews your renovation scope and requires contractor approval.

Path 3 — FHA 203k

Two versions. Streamlined (cosmetic work only, maximum $35,000 renovation) and Standard (any scope including structural, no cap). Both require 3.5% down and carry FHA mortgage insurance — 1.75% upfront rolled into the loan, plus 0.55% annually. On a $300,000 loan that's roughly $138 per month in ongoing MIP that lasts the life of the loan unless you refinance into conventional.

FHA 203k opens the door for buyers who don't have large down payments or strong credit. The tradeoff is MIP, rate premium, and more process complexity — HUD consultants, draw schedules, and required licensed contractors on the Standard version.

How the Neighborhood Picture Shifts

This is what the tool is actually doing — and why it exists. The fixer upper path doesn't just change your monthly payment. It changes which neighborhoods are in range.

A buyer at $80,000 income with $20,000 down has a move-in ready budget of approximately $285,000. Their move-in ready options are Oakville, Arnold, and parts of St. Charles County at that price point.

That same buyer using conventional + cash reserves, with a $45,000 moderate renovation budget, is shopping for a purchase price of roughly $240,000 — which opens Affton, Mehlville, and parts of South St. Louis City. Different neighborhoods, same total cost.

Using a HomeStyle renovation loan at +1%, the total loan is $285,000 — same neighborhoods as move-in ready, but they're buying a home that needs $45,000 of work and paying a higher rate for the privilege. In that scenario, the fixer upper path produces no neighborhood advantage.

A Note for VA Buyers

VA loans complicate the fixer upper path significantly. The VA requires move-in ready condition at appraisal — a home that needs a roof, has a Federal Pacific panel, or has foundation issues will fail the VA appraisal and the deal stops. VA renovation loans exist in theory but are rare in St. Louis. Most local lenders don't offer them.

VA buyers looking at fixer uppers should pre-screen every property before writing an offer. The list of common VA appraisal killers in the St. Louis market is specific and learnable.

When a Fixer Upper Actually Makes Sense

After 13 years and 250+ transactions, here's when the fixer upper math genuinely works in the St. Louis market:

  • You have realistic renovation scope. Not a wish list — a contractor-bid scope with a contingency budget. The buyers who struggle are the ones who discover the sewer lateral and the knob-and-tube wiring after closing.
  • The fixer upper opens a neighborhood you couldn't otherwise afford. If the total cost lands you in the same neighborhood as move-in ready, you're paying a rate premium for the pleasure of managing a renovation. The math only works if the neighborhood shifts.
  • You're not in a rush. A renovation loan with a draw schedule, a contractor who needs permits, and a kitchen that's non-functional for 10 weeks is a real disruption. Buyers who are already stretched thin on time or stress rarely describe it as worth it.
  • The property passed a pre-offer inspection. Not a full inspection — a walkthrough with a contractor you trust before you write the offer. It takes two hours and it changes what you know going in.

When It Doesn't

  • When the renovation loan rate premium erases the neighborhood advantage
  • When the fixer upper price already reflects the needed work (it usually does — other buyers walked for a reason)
  • When the scope is unknown at offer time
  • On a VA loan without pre-screening
23% regret rate 23% of fixer upper buyers say they would have chosen move-in ready if they could start over. The ones who don't regret it have one thing in common — they knew the scope before they closed.
