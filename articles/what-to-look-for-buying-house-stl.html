Pricing this house3

How do I tell if a house is priced above its like-kind sales?

Compare the list price with the middle of like-kind sales: the same kind of house, in the same school district, sold recently. Then adjust for condition. If it’s listed above where its condition puts it, the gap is your room to negotiate, especially if it’s been sitting. Run the check ↓

How does condition change what a house is worth compared with the comps?

Like-kind sales give you the range. Condition decides where in that range the house lands. If the house is older and original, the value moves toward the bottom of the range. If it was recently renovated, we’re looking at the top of the comparable homes.

How much can Zillow’s square footage overstate what a house is worth?

By the finished basement times the above-ground price per square foot. If like-kind homes sold for $220 a square foot above ground, counting a 500-square-foot finished basement at that rate adds $110,000 the comps don’t support. A finished basement adds value, just not at that rate. Basements and square footage →

Reading the house at a showing4

What does a permit sticker on the electrical panel tell me?

When the electrical system was updated. A new panel gets an electrical inspection, and the permit sticker shows it. I open the panel and look for one before I look at the brand and the breakers.

How can I tell how old the furnace, A/C and water heater are?

The furnace and A/C serial numbers tell you their age. Water heaters you can read by look: newer models have a sticker with the date, and the olive green tops are from the ’70s and ’80s.

How can I tell if the sewer line was replaced?

Look at the floor drain in the basement. A broken-out and new section of concrete around it means the line was dug up and replaced. Sometimes the stack is replaced and tied into the original cast iron. It adds context once the sewer inspection is done.

When did St. Louis houses switch from cast iron to plastic drain pipe?

Builders phased cast iron out for black plastic between about 1968 and 1972. Houses built before then started with cast iron, some 1970s houses still have it, and newer houses have plastic. Newer pipe means less risk, but still get the sewer inspection.

Walkthrough basics5

What should I look for when buying a house in St. Louis?

Start outside: the roof, gutters, tuckpointing, foundation cracks and grading. Then the basement for signs of water and the sewer cleanout, the ages of the furnace, AC and water heater, the electrical panel brand, the attic, and interior red flags like fresh paint everywhere, sticking doors and bouncy floors. The expensive problems are almost never the ones that catch your eye. Room-by-room checklist ↓

What is the most common expensive problem in St. Louis homes?

Sewer lateral failure is one of the most common expensive surprises. Clay tile laterals crack, offset, and get infiltrated by tree roots over decades. A camera inspection before closing costs $150 to $300. Replacement typically costs $4,000 to $12,000 and can exceed $15,000 in some cases. The second most common is basement water intrusion combined with failed tuckpointing on brick homes. The basement ↓

What are the signs of water in a basement?

White mineral deposits on the walls (efflorescence), staining or a waterline at the floor, patched or painted-over cracks and fresh basement paint. Find out where the water came from and what was done about it. The basement ↓

Should I get a sewer scope on every St. Louis home?

Yes, whatever its age. A scope runs $150 to $300. Home inspections in St. Louis →

How do I know if a house has a Federal Pacific panel?

Open the panel door: Federal Pacific or FPE on the label, “Stab-Lok” on the breakers. Homes built about 1950 to 1980 are the most likely to have one. Federal Pacific panels →

Exterior: What to Check Before You Enter

Most buyers start evaluating a home the moment they walk through the front door. I start in the driveway. The exterior tells you more about a home's maintenance history than anything inside, and in St. Louis specifically -- where brick construction dominates -- the outside is where the money lives.

Walking a house this weekend? Pull up my walk-through tool on your phone. Flag what you see room by room, and it adds up 2026 expected repair costs as you go. Save or email your results so you can compare houses later.

Use the walk-through tool →

  • Roof: Look for missing, curling, or granule-loss on asphalt shingles. A sagging ridge line indicates structural issues. Ask the listing agent how old the roof is -- if they do not know, that is information. Roofs in St. Louis typically last 20 to 25 years.
  • Gutters and downspouts: Gutters pulling away from the fascia or downspouts that terminate at the foundation are both water management failures that feed directly into basement problems.
  • Tuckpointing: On brick homes, run your finger along the mortar joints. Soft, crumbling, or recessed mortar needs attention. Failed tuckpointing is the primary cause of water intrusion in St. Louis brick homes -- and one of the most overlooked items during a casual showing.
  • Foundation: Horizontal cracks in a poured concrete foundation or stair-step cracks in block or brick indicate lateral pressure -- a more serious structural issue than vertical hairline cracks. Neither is automatically a dealbreaker, but both need professional evaluation.
  • Grading: The ground should slope away from the foundation on all sides. Negative grade -- where soil slopes toward the house -- channels water directly to the foundation and basement. Easy to miss, expensive to correct after the fact.
St. Louis Specific: Tuckpointing Brick is everywhere in older St. Louis neighborhoods, and mortar doesn’t last forever, so most older St. Louis brick homes need tuckpointing at some point. A full house tuckpointing runs $5,000 to $15,000. It is almost never disclosed upfront. Check every exterior brick wall.

Spotted something outside? Tap it to open the walk-through tool with it flagged: roof, tuckpointing, chimney, siding, windows or foundation. You’ll see the 2026 expected cost.

Basement

In St. Louis, the basement is where you find the expensive problems. Clay soil that expands when wet creates lateral pressure on basement walls. Old sewer laterals back up and fail. Water intrusion is common and ranges from cosmetic to structural. Take your time down here.

  • Efflorescence: White mineral deposits on basement walls indicate water has moved through the wall. It does not always mean active leaking but it means water has been present.
  • Staining at floor level: A waterline on the wall or staining at the wall-floor junction indicates past water intrusion. Ask when it happened and what was done.
  • Sump pump: Is there one? How old? Does the pit have water in it? A functioning sump pump is actively managing water -- which is fine, but you want to understand what it is managing.
  • Previous repairs: Hydraulic cement patches, painted-over cracks, or fresh paint on basement walls can indicate attempts to manage water issues. Not always -- but note it and ask.
  • Sewer lateral access: Look for a cleanout access point -- a capped pipe, usually 3 to 4 inches in diameter, near the floor. On every St. Louis home, budget $150 to $300 for a camera inspection of the lateral before you close. Clay tile laterals crack, offset, and collapse. Replacement typically runs $4,000 to $12,000 and can exceed $15,000 in some cases.

Water or sewer questions? Flag basement waterproofing, the sewer lateral or foundation repair in the walk-through tool, and get the scope during your inspection.

Mechanical Systems

  • HVAC age: The manufacture date is printed on the data plate inside the unit. Furnaces over 15 years and AC condensers over 12 years are approaching end of service life. A full system replacement in St. Louis runs $7,000 to $15,000. Factor this into your offer.
  • Water heater age: Same principle -- the manufacture date is on the data plate. Standard tank water heaters last 8 to 12 years. Replacement runs $900 to $1,800 installed.
  • Electrical panel: Look for the panel brand. Federal Pacific Stab-Lok panels and Zinsco panels are known fire hazard brands that should be replaced. These show up frequently in St. Louis homes from the 1960s and 70s. Panel replacement runs $2,000 to $4,000. Count the circuits -- a 100-amp panel in a large home is undersized for modern use.
  • Plumbing supply lines: Original galvanized steel supply lines restrict water pressure and corrode from the inside. Look under sinks and in the mechanical room. If you see grey steel pipe, ask when the supply lines were updated.
  • Cast iron drain stack: Builders phased cast iron drain pipe out for plastic between about 1968 and 1972, so most St. Louis homes built before then have original cast iron drain stacks, and some 1970s homes still do. Cast iron isn’t a problem by itself, but it deteriorates with age and can develop cracks, scale buildup and joint failures. A camera inspection of the main drain stack is a reasonable ask on any house with cast iron.
Federal Pacific Panels in St. Louis Federal Pacific Electric Stab-Lok panels were installed in millions of U.S. homes from the 1950s into the 1980s. Multiple fire investigations have linked them to breaker failure and house fires. They appear frequently in St. Louis homes built in that era. If the listing agent or seller does not know what type of panel a home has, read the label yourself before you leave the showing.

Old systems? Flag the HVAC, water heater, electrical panel or supply plumbing in the walk-through tool to see what replacement would cost.

Attic

  • Insulation: Visible rafters with no insulation or compressed, inadequate insulation means higher utility costs. St. Louis winters are not mild -- proper insulation matters.
  • Ventilation: Proper ridge and soffit ventilation prevents moisture buildup that causes sheathing rot and mold growth.
  • Staining or daylight: Water staining on sheathing indicates past or active roof leaks. Visible daylight means the roof covering has failed somewhere.
  • Knob-and-tube wiring: In homes built before 1940, original knob-and-tube wiring may still be present in the attic. Most insurers will not cover homes with active knob-and-tube wiring, and many lenders require its removal before closing.

Found knob-and-tube or roof leaks? Flag a full rewire or a roof replacement in the walk-through tool.

Interior Red Flags

  • Fresh paint over everything: A full interior repaint -- especially in a basement or on ceilings -- is worth a second look. It does not mean the seller is hiding something, but it means you cannot see what was underneath.
  • Doors and windows that do not operate: Sticking doors and windows can indicate foundation movement or framing settlement. Check every door and every operable window.
  • Floor bounce: Walk across wood floors and feel for bounce or flex. This can indicate damaged or undersized floor joists below, or active wood rot.
  • Ceiling stains: Brown staining on ceilings, especially in upper-level rooms directly below a roof, indicates past or active roof leaks.
  • Radon: Missouri has elevated radon levels in many areas. A radon test runs $100 to $150 and is worth adding during the inspection period. Mitigation systems run $800 to $1,500 if needed.

Cosmetic work adds up too. Flag paint, drywall, flooring, a kitchen update or a bath update in the walk-through tool, then save or email your results to compare houses.

What This Means For Your Offer

This checklist is not designed to talk you out of buying a house. It is designed to make sure the price you offer reflects what is actually there -- not just what the staging and fresh paint make you feel.

Every item on this list has a cost range. Understanding which items are present and roughly what they will cost to address gives you the information to make a defensible offer and negotiate from a position of knowledge rather than hope.

The goal going into a showing is not to find a perfect house -- those are rare. The goal is to understand what you are buying well enough to price it correctly and know when to walk.

More Questions About What to Look For

Outside

How can I tell if a roof needs to be replaced?

Look for missing or curling shingles, granule loss and a sagging ridge line, and ask how old the roof is. St. Louis roofs typically last 20 to 25 years. An asphalt roof replacement runs $8,000 to $20,000 in 2026 expected costs. Exterior ↓

Which foundation cracks are serious?

Horizontal cracks in poured concrete and stair-step cracks in block or brick point to lateral pressure, which is more serious than vertical hairline cracks. Neither is automatically a deal-breaker, but both need a professional evaluation. Should I buy a house with foundation cracks? →

What does negative grading mean and why does it matter?

Negative grading means the ground slopes toward the foundation instead of away from it. This channels rain and snowmelt directly against the foundation and into the basement. Correcting it runs $500 to $3,000 in 2026 expected costs, from simple soil regrading to more significant drainage work. It is one of the easiest things to spot during an exterior walkthrough and one of the most overlooked.

Is tuckpointing always a dealbreaker on a St. Louis brick home?

No -- most older St. Louis brick homes have tuckpointing that is at various stages of wear. The question is whether it has failed to the point of allowing water intrusion, and how much work needs to be done. A single failing facade is a $1,500 to $4,000 repair. A full house that has not been touched in 30 years is a $5,000 to $15,000 project. Factor the scope into your offer rather than treating it as automatic grounds to walk.

Inside

What should I do if a house has knob-and-tube wiring?

Plan on replacing it. Most insurers won’t cover a home with active knob-and-tube wiring, and many lenders require it to be removed before closing. Look for it in the attic of homes built before 1940. The attic ↓

Should I test for radon?

Yes. Missouri has elevated radon in many areas. A test runs $100 to $150 during the inspection period, and a mitigation system runs $800 to $1,500 if you need one.
