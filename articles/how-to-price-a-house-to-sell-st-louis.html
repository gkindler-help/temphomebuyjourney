How to Price a House to Sell in St. Louis: Price It Right the First Time or End Up Below It

Last updated October 30, 2026

In 2026, St. Louis County houses that sold in their first two weeks were listed just under what comparable houses sold for, and buyers bid them above it. Houses that sat 60+ days started about 4% over comparable houses and finished about 5% under them. Overpricing doesn’t just cost time. It costs the money you were trying to get.

Pricing a House to Sell, Answered

How should I price my house to sell in St. Louis?

At what the comparable sales that actually won support, for the buyers shopping now. In 2026, County houses that sold within 14 days were listed at a median 97.1% of comparable houses’ price per square foot and sold at 101.0%. Pricing for the first two weeks →

What does overpricing a house cost?

In 2026, County houses that sat 60+ days were listed at a median 104.0% of comparable houses and sold at 94.6%. On a house worth $300,000, that’s about $19,000 less than pricing it right, plus about three months of carrying costs. The two paths in dollars →

Is it better to price it right from the start?

Yes. In 2026, the 2,235 County sellers who never cut their price sold for a median 101.5% of their original list in a median 5 days, and 58.6% sold above asking. The 630 who cut sold for a median 91.6%, and only 15 got back to their original price. What a price cut gets you →

Should I price below market to start a bidding war?

Not deliberately. The houses that sold fastest were listed at the comps, a median 97.1% of comparable houses, not far below them, and buyers took them above list. I price for the strongest reason to act, not to manufacture multiple offers. Underpricing vs. pricing at the comps →

Will $299,900 sell faster than $305,000?

Not by itself. In 2026 County sales, houses listed just under a $25,000 mark sold in a median 6 days and houses at or just over one sold in 7, with 60% of each selling within two weeks. Where the price sits against the comps matters more than how it ends. The price-ending numbers →

Should I hire the agent who says my house is worth the most?

Not on that number alone. Ask what evidence is behind it and what happens if buyers don’t respond. In 2026, houses that started about 4% over comparable houses and sat sold about 5% under them. Choosing an agent by price →

No. Price it from the comparable sales that competed for the same buyers, adjusted for season and condition. The Zestimate in St. Louis →

How do you price a house to sell in the first two weeks?

Start from the comparable sales that won, and how they got there. A house that listed at $400,000, sat two months, cut twice and sold for $370,000 tells you something very different from one that listed at $375,000 and drew competition the first weekend. Then price for the buyers shopping now, against the houses they can buy instead of yours.

The 2026 County sales show what that looks like. Houses that sold within 14 days were listed just under comparable houses and sold just over them. The longer a house sat, the higher it had started and the lower it finished.

What does overpricing a house cost you?

More than the time. Take a house whose comparable sales support $300,000. At the 2026 County medians, priced to sell it lists around $291,000 and sells around $303,000 in a few days. Priced high and left to sit 60+ days, it lists around $312,000 and sells around $284,000, three months later.

What does it cost to price high and sit?

Uses the 2026 St. Louis County medians in the table above: houses that sold within 14 days listed at 97.1% of comparable houses and sold at 101.0% in a median 4 days; houses that sat 60+ days listed at 104.0% and sold at 94.6% in a median of about 90 days, which is why months defaults to 3. It shows the typical pattern, not a prediction for your house.

Does a price cut fix an overpriced house?

Rarely all the way. A price cut brings buyers back, but it doesn’t rewind the market. The buyers who saw the house at the old price have already moved on or bought something else, and the new ones see how long it’s been listed.

Should you underprice your house to start a bidding war?

Not on purpose. Pricing at the comps already draws the competition: 66.8% of County houses that sold within 14 days in 2026 went above their original list. Pricing far below the comps to manufacture multiple offers can leave money on the table if only one buyer shows up, and it’s not something I do to brag about offer counts.

From one of my sales: A Fenton house listed at $295,000 in November drew about 20 showings and three offers at $300,000, and a cash buyer’s escalation clause took it to $302,000 with no repair requests. It was priced at what the comps supported, in the slowest season, and buyers did the rest. Read the sale →

Does $299,900 sell faster than $305,000?

Not in the 2026 data. Of County houses listed between $150,000 and $750,000, the ones listed just under a $25,000 mark ($299,900, $324,900 and so on) sold in a median 6 days, and the ones at or just over a mark sold in 7. In both groups, 60% sold within two weeks, and both sold at about 100% of their original list.

Search filters do matter at the edges: a buyer who caps a search at $300,000 won’t see a house at $305,000. But the price ending can’t rescue a house priced over its comps, and a house priced at its comps sells either way.

What can’t these numbers tell you about your house?

Pricing isn’t the only reason houses sit. Some of the houses that sat had problems the data can’t see: a busy road, a wet basement, poor photos, a seller who wasn’t ready to move. So part of the gap between the fast and slow houses is the house, not just the price. The direction held at every step, though: the longer houses sat, the higher they had started and the lower they finished.
