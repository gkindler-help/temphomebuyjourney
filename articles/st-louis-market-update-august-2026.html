St. Louis Housing Market Update: August 2026

Every few weeks a new headline tells St. Louis buyers something different — prices are crashing, prices are soaring, rates are about to fall, rates will never fall. After more than 250 transactions across this metro, here's what the August numbers actually say, in plain terms, and what they mean if you're trying to buy a home right now.

By George Kindler · Licensed Missouri Real Estate Agent · The Closing Pros LLC

Let's start with the three figures that shape almost every buyer decision this month: mortgage rates, prices, and how long homes are taking to sell.

None of those three is dramatic on its own. Together, though, they describe a market that has quietly changed character from the one buyers were bracing for a year ago. The panic scenarios — a crash, or a return to 3% rates — are both off the table. What's left is something more workable, if you understand it.

Mortgage Rates: Stable, Not Falling Off a Cliff

As of August 20, 2026, Freddie Mac put the average 30-year fixed mortgage rate at 6.65%, down slightly from 6.67% the week before. That's roughly where rates have sat for most of 2026 — drifting inside a narrow mid-6% band rather than making any big move in either direction.

Here's the part that matters for your planning: nearly every serious forecaster expects rates to stay above 6% for the foreseeable future rather than dropping back to the 3–4% levels of a few years ago. If your entire buying plan is built around waiting for a dramatic rate drop, you may be waiting on something that isn't coming. The more useful move is to plan around today's rate — and I'll explain in a moment why waiting for a drop can actually work against you.

Prices: Still Rising, But Gently

Despite the crash headlines, St. Louis home prices are not falling. Across the metro, most measures show modest, low-single-digit appreciation compared to a year ago. That's a healthy, sustainable pace — the kind that builds equity over time without locking first-time buyers out entirely.

St. Louis has always been more stable than the coastal and Sunbelt markets that swing wildly. We didn't get the extreme pandemic boom those cities saw, and we're not getting their correction either. What we have instead is steady ground. For a buyer, steady ground is a good thing: it means you're far less likely to buy today and watch your value collapse next year.

One caution on the numbers themselves. You'll see "median price" figures quoted anywhere from the low $250,000s to over $300,000 depending on the source — and they can all be right at once. City of St. Louis proper runs lower than St. Louis County, which runs different from the broader metro. Monthly medians also bounce around based on which homes happened to sell that month. Don't anchor on a single number from a single headline; the trend matters more than the snapshot.

Inventory and Days on Market: Buyers Have Room to Breathe

This is the biggest shift from a year ago. There are simply more homes to choose from, and they're staying on the market longer. The frantic pace — fifteen offers in a weekend, waived inspections, sight-unseen bids — has eased across most of the metro.

For you, that translates into real, practical leverage that buyers haven't had in years:

  • Inspection contingencies are back. You can once again make an offer contingent on a proper inspection without automatically losing the house. That protection alone can save you tens of thousands.
  • There's room to negotiate. On homes that have sat a few weeks, sellers are far more open to price, repairs, or closing-cost help than they were in the frenzy.
  • You can actually think. You no longer have to decide on the biggest purchase of your life in a four-hour window. That's not a small thing.

The Catch: St. Louis Has Two Markets Right Now

Here's what a single "days on market" number hides. St. Louis doesn't really have one housing market anymore — it has two, running side by side, and they behave completely differently.

Market #1: Move-In-Ready Homes

Updated kitchens, solid roofs, modern systems, little deferred maintenance. These are the homes most buyers actually want, and they still move quickly — sometimes with multiple offers — in strong areas like South County, Oakville, Affton, Mehlville, Arnold, Kirkwood, and St. Charles County. Buyers are paying a premium for certainty: for a home that won't need $30,000 of work the month after closing.

Market #2: Homes That Need Work

Older roofs, dated interiors, aging HVAC, deferred maintenance. This is where most of the inventory growth is actually happening, and where the longer days-on-market numbers come from. Fewer buyers today have the cash or appetite to take on a big project on top of a 6.65% mortgage, so these homes sit, drop their prices, and pad the inventory count.

This split is why the market can feel tight and loose at the same time. The overall inventory number is up — but if you're hunting for a turnkey home in a good school district, it may still feel competitive, because everyone else wants that same short list of homes. Knowing which market a given house lives in is half the battle.

Why "Just Wait for Rates to Drop" Is Risky Advice

This is the single most common thing I hear from buyers on the fence: I'll wait until rates come down. It sounds sensible. It can quietly backfire.

When rates fall, they don't fall just for you. Every one of the thousands of buyers currently sitting on the sidelines is watching the exact same headlines, waiting for the exact same signal. The day rates drop, they all re-enter the market at once. Demand jumps, competition returns, and prices get bid up — often erasing the monthly savings the lower rate was supposed to give you.

You marry the house and date the rate, as the saying goes. If you buy a home you can afford today and rates fall later, you can refinance into that lower rate — while the buyer who waited is now competing with a crowd for the same house. The real risk usually isn't buying before everyone else. It's buying with everyone else.

So — Is Now a Good Time to Buy in St. Louis?

The honest answer is that it depends far less on the market than on you. After 250-plus closings, the buyers who do well aren't the ones who timed the market perfectly. They're the ones who bought a home they could comfortably afford, in a location that fit their life, and stayed long enough for the equity to build. Three questions matter more than any headline:

  • Can you comfortably afford the monthly payment at today's rate — not stretched to the ceiling?
  • Do you plan to stay put long enough (usually 5+ years) to absorb the transaction costs?
  • Have you found a home that genuinely fits your needs, not just your budget?

If the answer to those is yes, the exact rate this month matters a lot less than it feels like it does. If the answer is no, no rate is low enough to make it the right move. That's the part the headlines never tell you.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the average mortgage rate in August 2026?

As of August 20, 2026, Freddie Mac reported the average 30-year fixed mortgage rate at 6.65%, down slightly from 6.67% the week before. Rates have held in the mid-6% range through most of 2026, and most forecasters expect them to stay above 6% rather than returning to pandemic-era lows.

Are home prices going up or down in St. Louis in 2026?

They're still rising modestly — not falling. Most measures show low-single-digit year-over-year appreciation across the metro. Prices vary widely by geography and by home condition: move-in-ready homes in desirable areas hold value and sell quickly, while homes needing work sit longer and see reductions.

Is it a buyer's or seller's market in St. Louis right now?

It's shifting toward balance. Inventory has grown and homes take longer to sell than during the recent frenzy, which gives buyers more room to negotiate and to use inspection contingencies again. But well-priced, move-in-ready homes in strong areas still draw competition, so it depends heavily on the specific property.

Should I wait for lower mortgage rates before buying in St. Louis?

Waiting carries its own risk. When rates drop, sidelined buyers tend to re-enter the market all at once, increasing competition and pushing prices up. For many buyers, purchasing a home they can afford now and refinancing later means less competition than waiting for the crowd.
