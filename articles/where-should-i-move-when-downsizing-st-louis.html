Where Should I Move When Downsizing in St. Louis?

Maybe downsizing is the wrong way to think about it. Your next house doesn't necessarily need to be smaller. It needs to fit the life you're living now.

I recently helped my clients Roy and Karen buy a beautiful home in Fenton. You could look at the move they made and call it downsizing.

I think right-sizing describes it better.

Roy and Karen bought their longtime home in the Carondelet area of South St. Louis before they had kids. It's a 100+ year-old two-story just north of Bates.

They raised their kids there. They loved being close to their church and Woodward. For a long time, the house fit the life they were living.

Then life changed.

Their kids grew up. Their kids had children of their own. Their family started putting down roots in Fenton. Attendance at their church declined and eventually the church closed.

Little by little, their routine changed.

Their house didn't.

About three months ago, we started looking for their next home. And we weren't really searching for something smaller.

We were searching for something that fit their life now.

They wanted to be close to their grandchildren. Karen wanted space for music and crafts. Roy wanted a garage where he could work on his car. They wanted some green space out back where the grandkids could play. And moving from a two-story home into a ranch made sense for the years ahead.

They eventually found it in Fenton: a ranch with a large unfinished basement, a backyard for the grandkids, a garage for Roy and, most importantly, a much shorter trip to the people they wanted to be around.

The funny thing?

Their new house is roughly the same size as their old house.

They didn't really downsize.

They right-sized.

Don't Ask Where You Should Move Yet

Before we start talking about Fenton, Oakville, condos, villas, ranches or any other part of St. Louis, answer a different question:

What do you want your next home to make easier?

That's what Roy and Karen figured out. Their priorities weren't buying fewer square feet, eliminating a certain number of bedrooms or finding a “retirement community.” Their priorities were family, hobbies, the garage, outdoor space and a house that worked better for the next stage of their lives.

Your list might look completely different. Maybe you're tired of taking care of a big yard. Maybe the stairs are becoming something you're thinking about. Maybe you want to walk to restaurants and coffee shops. Maybe your kids moved across the region and you're tired of the drive. Maybe you want to travel without worrying about the house. Or maybe you love your community and simply want a house that's easier to maintain.

Those answers should determine where you look. Not your age, and not somebody else's idea of what downsizing is supposed to look like.

Right-Sizing Is About Trade-Offs

There probably isn't a house that gives you everything your current home has, eliminates everything you don't like about it and costs substantially less. You're going to make some trades. The important part is deciding which trades are worth making.

Maybe you'll accept a smaller yard to eliminate maintenance. Maybe you'll pay an HOA fee because you'd rather write a check than spend Saturday cutting grass. Maybe you'll spend more on a smaller ranch because one-level living and proximity to your grandchildren matter more than square footage.

Or maybe you'll run through the trade-offs and realize: I actually like where I am.

That's a perfectly legitimate answer too.

The goal isn't to downsize. The goal is to get the house, location and lifestyle aligned again.

Start With the People

If you've lived in the same house for 20 or 30 years, think about where the important people in your life were when you bought it. Now think about where they are today. Those might be two completely different maps.

Your children may have moved. Grandchildren may now be pulling you toward a part of St. Louis you never would have considered when you were raising your own kids. Friends and other family may have moved too.

That's what happened with Roy and Karen. Fenton wasn't important because somebody put it on a list of the “best places to downsize.” It was important because their grandchildren were there.

Start there. Who do you want to be closer to? And just as importantly, who or what don't you want to move farther away from?

Then Look at Your Routine

Your house is only one part of where you live. There's also the life you've built around it.

Where's your doctor? Where do you go to church? Where do you buy groceries? Where do you meet friends? Where are your hobbies? How often do you see your family?

You don't necessarily need to stay close to every one of those things. Some may not matter anymore. That's part of the exercise.

Roy and Karen's old location made sense when their church was an important part of their routine and their children were growing up in South City. Years later, some of those anchors had changed.

Has your routine changed while your address stayed the same?

If it has, your next location should reflect the life you're living now—not the life you were living when you bought your current house.

What Do You Actually Need From the House?

Forget square footage for a minute. What do you actually do at home?

Roy works on his car. Karen enjoys music and crafts. They wanted room for their grandchildren. Those activities require space.

Buying a dramatically smaller home just because that's what people are “supposed” to do could have made their next house less useful than the one they were leaving.

Maybe you need a home office. Maybe you host Thanksgiving. Maybe woodworking requires a basement or garage. Maybe you have a dog and don't want to lose a fenced yard. Maybe grandchildren stay overnight. Or maybe you barely use anything beyond your bedroom, kitchen and living room.

Don't count bedrooms yet. List what you actually do. Then find the house that supports it.

What Are You Tired of Taking Care Of?

Now make the opposite list. What don't you want anymore?

Maybe it's the yard. Maybe it's two flights of stairs. Maybe it's an old house that always seems to need another project. Maybe it's thousands of square feet of cleaning. Or maybe you love gardening and would hate giving up the yard.

There's no universal list of things you're supposed to eliminate. Identify the responsibilities you no longer want.

That's where housing type starts to matter. A ranch might solve the stairs without changing much else. A villa might reduce exterior maintenance while still feeling like a house. A condo could eliminate even more maintenance but introduce association fees, shared spaces and rules. Renting could remove many ownership responsibilities entirely.

Those aren't automatically better or worse choices. They're different sets of trade-offs.

Think About the House You'll Need Ten Years From Now

There's another person who gets a vote in this decision: you, ten years from now.

You don't need to predict everything that might happen. But if you're moving anyway, it's reasonable to think beyond what works today.

A staircase that doesn't bother you today might matter more later. A large yard you enjoy maintaining today might eventually become something you're paying someone else to maintain. A main-floor bedroom and full bathroom may give you options a traditional two-story doesn't.

That doesn't mean buying a house around every possible future limitation. It means your next house should ideally give you more options, not fewer.

Now We Can Talk About Where in St. Louis

Once you know what you're looking for, geography becomes much easier.

Instead of asking, Where should I move when downsizing in St. Louis?, ask:

Where can I find the kind of life and house I just described?

If staying near your current community matters most, start close to home and see whether the housing stock gives you better options. If family is the priority, start around them and work outward. If budget drives the decision, compare what your money actually buys in different parts of the St. Louis area. If you're specifically looking for ranches, villas or condos, the map may change again.

You don't need a list telling you the “best” place to retire. You need enough information to determine which places fit your priorities.

Don't Forget the Money

Right-sizing your life doesn't automatically right-size your expenses.

A smaller house isn't necessarily a cheaper house. You may own your current home outright or have a very small mortgage. The replacement house will be purchased in today's market.

Then there may be selling expenses, moving costs, repairs or updates, HOA or condo fees, different taxes, different insurance and potentially a new mortgage.

You have to compare the whole situation.

If selling your current house and buying the next one leaves you with substantial cash and lower ongoing expenses, that's meaningful. If you're going to spend tens of thousands of dollars moving to save a relatively small amount each month, that's meaningful too.

Neither number tells you what to do. But you should know the numbers before deciding.

The Right-Sizing Test

Before you start seriously looking at houses, answer six questions: Who do I want to be closer to? What routines matter? What do I actually need from the house? What am I tired of maintaining? What should be easier ten years from now? And what does the money need to accomplish?

You can work through those questions below and turn them into a simple search profile.

Use the Profile to Build Your Search

Your profile isn't a verdict. It's a filter.

Take the things that matter most and divide them into three groups: must have, would be nice, and don't want anymore.

Must HaveThe things that make moving worthwhile: perhaps family proximity, a main-floor bedroom, a two-car garage or a hard monthly-cost limit.
Would Be NiceThings you'd like but could compromise on: a finished basement, a larger yard, an extra bedroom or a particular community.
Don't Want AnymoreThe responsibilities you're moving away from: stairs, a huge lawn, constant projects, unused rooms or a long drive to family.
Don't Accidentally LoseThe things your current house gives you that still matter: storage, hobby space, privacy, a garage, gathering space or a location you love.

Now we actually have something useful to search for.

Not: “Find me a smaller house.”

Instead: “Find me the house that makes the next part of my life work better.”

You May Not Need to Downsize at All

After going through this exercise, you may discover that your current house still fits your life pretty well.

Maybe hiring someone to handle the lawn solves the biggest problem. Maybe changing how you use the first floor makes the house work differently. Maybe a few targeted improvements let you comfortably stay for years.

Or maybe you realize the house isn't really the problem.

If that's where the exercise takes you, that's useful information. Selling a longtime home is too big of a decision to make simply because you've reached an age where people start asking whether you're going to downsize.

Your Next House Should Fit the Life You're Living Now

Roy and Karen didn't move because a spreadsheet told them they had too much square footage.

Their life changed. Their family moved. Their routines changed. Eventually, they decided their house should change with them.

They didn't give up the things that mattered just to say they downsized.

They found room for music. Room for crafts. A garage for working on the car. A backyard for the grandkids. One-level living. And a home closer to their family.

That's why I think right-sizing is a much better way to think about this.

You aren't trying to win an award for owning the smallest house. You're trying to decide what you want the next ten or twenty years of your life to look like—and then find a home that helps you live it.

Start with the life. Then find the house.
